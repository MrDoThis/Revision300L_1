Master Question Guide & Module Breakdown

MODULE 1: Foundations, Etymology, and Components of Communication

Q1.1: Relate the root meaning of 'communication' with its core components and explain why "To live is to communicate and to communicate is to live."

  • Etymology & Root Meaning: The word "communication" originates from the Latin root communis, which translates to "to share". In communication, meaning is the central key; it represents the process of sharing knowledge, ideas, beliefs, and understandings. Something must be common among the participants for communication to occur.

  • The Saying Explained: Aina (2003) emphasizes that communication is as fundamental as human life itself, leading to the American axiom: "You cannot not communicate". Because every human action, reaction, silence, or posture conveys meaning to our physical and psychological environment, communication is inescapable and coterminous with existence.

  • Key Components of Communication: To complete the communication process, five core elements are required:

    1. Sender (Encoder): The originator of the message who packages the idea into recognizable codes or symbols for the target audience.

    2. Message: The central idea, information, action, or reaction that prompts the sender and joins the components together.

    3. Medium: The channel, pathway, or technology through which the message travels from sender to receiver.

    4. Receiver (Decoder): The target end-user who attributes meaning to the message and interprets the codified symbols.

    5. Feedback: The receiver's response or reaction sent back to the sender, turning the transmission into a dynamic, two-way loop.

MODULE 2: Conceptualizing International Communication & Study Rationale

Q2.1: Define International Communication and discuss the core factors that necessitate its study.

  • Definitions:

    • Aina defines it as communication exchanges or interactions transcending national, political, cultural, and economic boundaries, facilitated by the interdependency of nations, groups, and individuals.

    • Ahuja (1978) defines it as the communication process between different countries or nations across frontiers.

    • Prosser (1978) views it as any interaction taking place across national political boundaries.

    • Markham and Taylor frame it as people speaking to people and governments speaking to governments across different geographical regions.

  • Core Agreement Criteria: International communication requires crossing national boundaries, involving parties of different territories, addressing matters of international value/interest, and utilizing international channels or languages.

  • Rationale for Study:

    1. Enhancement of Peaceful Coexistence: Following devastating world wars, early studies (such as Harold Lasswell's propaganda analysis and the Hutchins Commission report of 1946) established that abundant, true, and cheap information fosters cross-cultural understanding and reduces international conflicts.

    2. Mutual Development (Multifaceted):

      • Ideological Development: Spreads political and economic systems like democracy and capitalism.

      • Political & Economic Development: Facilitates bilateral/multilateral policy borrowing, global trade, and economic strategies (e.g., global banking solutions).

      • Educational & Technological Development: Breaks geographical boundaries for research, knowledge sharing, and global innovations.

      • Social & Legal Development: Fosters global socialization via social networks and establishes judicial frameworks like the International Court of Justice (ICJ) to prevent international conflicts.

MODULE 3: Historical Development of International Communication

Q3.1: Trace the historical evolution of international communication from oral epochs to the digital new media era.

  • Oral Communication (The Age of Innocence): Early human interaction relied on gestures and primitive vocalizations. Limited by tribal or village boundaries, oral traditions kept civilizations isolated where "the world beyond the next mountain was largely unknown".

  • Development of Writing and Printing: Ancient rulers (Greek, Roman, Persian, Chinese) used inscriptions and dispatch runners (horsemen) to project administrative control. The introduction of paper from China and Gutenberg’s printing press in the 15th/16th centuries allowed mass duplication of texts in colonial languages (Portuguese, Spanish, English, French), driving imperial expansion.

  • Telegraph and Telephone: Samuel Morse’s electric telegraph (1837) and underwater cables (transatlantic cable in 1866) enabled instantaneous, secure, coded communication, cementing British and European imperial administration. Alexander Graham Bell’s invention of the telephone (1876) further revolutionized real-time interpersonal connectivity.

  • The Birth of Radio: Following wireless experiments by Guglielmo Marconi and the accidental launch of KDKA in 1916, radio emerged as a cheap, mass-scale technology capable of transcending political and geographic borders, prompting international frequency allocations (Berlin Conference, 1906).

  • The Emergence of Television: Combining elements of theater, film, and radio, television evolved through mechanical (Paul Nipkow, John Logie Baird) and electronic systems (cathode ray tube by Karl Braun, Zworykin, and Philo Farnsworth in the 1920s-1930s) into a global visual medium.

  • The New Media Era: Marked by computers, the internet, and digital networks, new media offers on-demand, interactive, decentralized access, breaking the physical barrier of distance and transforming traditional print and broadcast workflows.

MODULE 4: Nature, Characteristics, and Modes of International Communication

Q4.1: Identify and explain the core characteristics of international communication and provide examples of activities that compel it.

  • Characteristics (Fortner, 1993):

    1. Intentionality: Can be intentional (purposely directed to foreign audiences, like Voice of Nigeria - VON) or unintentional (signal spillover due to geographic proximity).

    2. Channels: Utilizes both public channels (state broadcasters) and private commercial channels.

    3. Distribution Technology: Employs standard media technologies (radio, TV, internet) equipped with extended power to cross international boundaries.

    4. Content: Features public affairs, sports, or news, but framed with an international outlook and global relevance.

    5. Cultural Dominance: Frequently reflects the cultural standards, values, and ideologies of global powers over sub-cultures.

  • Activities Compelling International Communication:

    • International Trade: Export-import transactions and global services.

    • Wars & Crises: Geopolitical conflicts (e.g., Cold War, US-Iraq war).

    • Health Issues: Epidemics of global concern (e.g., Ebola, HIV/AIDS, Influenza).

    • Educational Activities: International summits, academic conferences, and global research dialogues.

    • Religious & Social Activities: Holy pilgrimages (Mecca) and international tourism/festivals.

    • Games & Sports: Global mega-events like the FIFA World Cup, Olympic Games, and continental leagues.

MODULE 5: International Media Systems, Forms, Ownership, and Control

Q5.1: Contrast local and international media ownership structures and discuss external mechanisms of media control.

  • Local Media Ownership Structures:

    • Public Ownership: Completely owned and funded by the state; used as an ideological apparatus under communist or totalitarian regimes.

    • Private Ownership: Owned by private individuals/investors in capitalist, free-market economies.

    • Joint Ownership: A hybrid system combining public and private ownership under regulatory frameworks (common in mixed economies like Nigeria).

  • International Media Ownership: Characterized by corporate concentration, media oligopolies, horizontal/vertical integrations, and transnational conglomerates (e.g., Disney, Sony) that prioritize global profits.

  • Forms of International Media Control:

    • Internal Control: Exercised by corporate owners, board directors, interlocking directorates, and editorial staff whose outputs conform to institutional and financial interests.

    • External Controls:

      1. Government Control: State censorship, licensing restrictions, and targeted advertising boycotts against critical outlets.

      2. International Organizations: Global treaties and spectrum allocations (e.g., UN, ITU, AU frameworks) governing trans-border signals.

      3. Advertisers' Control: Commercial dependence on corporate advertisers forces outlets to skew content to avoid offending financial backers.

      4. Legal & Audience Control: Compliance with national/international media laws and audience consumer preferences shaping media output.

      5. Competitors' Control: Standards set by elite global news leaders force competing agencies to upgrade verification and output quality.

MODULE 6: Ideologies, Cultural Globalization, and Intercultural Communication

Q6.1: Discuss how international communication drives the homogenization of global culture through Americanization and Westernization.

  • Media Ideology & Hegemony: Media texts carry underlying belief systems and worldviews defined by dominant ruling classes. Global media systems reflect the macro-hegemony of Western economic, political, and technological powers.

  • Americanization: The pervasive influence of United States culture, lifestyle, and media products (Hollywood films, popular music, CNN, HBO) substituted into foreign societies, often creating cultural dependency.

  • Westernization: The broader historical process where non-Western societies adopt Western structures in technology, industry, law, politics, and consumerism through acculturation and colonial expansion.

  • Intercultural Communication Principles: Guidelines for exchanging meaningful information across cultural divides with mutual respect, recognizing that globalization does not create a completely homogeneous market, but interacts complexly with local traditions.

MODULE 7: International Media Networks, Coverage, and Reach

Q7.1: Identify the criteria that make a media station "international" and explain the barriers to international media coverage.

  • Criteria for an International Station:

    1. Non-Culturally Bound Content: Programming must appeal across diverse cultural domains rather than serving a single alien culture.

    2. Regional/Global Network: Distribution reach must extend far beyond the originating region via satellites, cables, or digital streaming.

    3. International Language: Broadcasts must utilize globally understood languages (English, French, Arabic, Spanish) rather than restricted local dialects.

  • Barriers to International Media Coverage:

    1. Language differences and translation gaps.

    2. Restrictive government policies and state censorship.

    3. International regulatory policies and frequency allocations.

    4. Corporate ownership and editorial filter policies.

    5. Cultural differences and local sensitivities.

    6. Economic disparities and high infrastructure costs.

    7. Legal considerations, libel laws, and trans-border copyright restrictions.

MODULE 8: International News Sources, Flow Patterns, and Audiences

Q8.1: Analyze the three major patterns of international news flow and evaluate their impact on developing nations.

  • Major International News Sources: Government briefings, international news correspondents/freelancers, internet platforms/satellites, local/national media outlets, eyewitnesses/interviewees, and victim/battlefield remnants.

  • Patterns of International News Flow:

    1. Vertical News Flow (Top-Down / Bottom-Up): A master-servant information structure where powerful Western nations at the top dictate news content, leading to skewed, imbalanced reportage, negative stereotyping, and cultural dominance over developing nations at the bottom.

    2. Horizontal News Flow: Information sharing between nations of equal economic and media strength, ensuring balanced representation, mutual voice, and zero propaganda dominance.

    3. Horizo-Vertical (Mixed) Flow: A transitional model attempting to balance coverage, though major wire services still heavily skew reporting toward conflict and crises in developing regions.

  • Demographics & Audience Measurement: International media producers analyze audience demographics (income brackets A-E, age, gender, needs) and measure reach via box office receipts, print circulation, broadcast ratings (e.g., BARB), and internet web hit metrics.

MODULE 9: International News Agencies and Operations

Q9.1: Compare notable international news agencies (AP, AFP, ITAR-TASS, Reuters, UPI, and PANA) and their operational models.

  • Associated Press (AP): An American cooperative news agency owned by contributing US newspapers and broadcasters; operates over 243 bureaus across 121 countries with a factual "just-the-facts" writing style.

  • Agence France-Presse (AFP): Descended from Agence Havas (1835); restructured in 1944 under strict statutory independence from government or ideological control; operates extensive global multimedia bureaus in 65 countries.

  • ITAR-TASS: Russia's central state information agency (succeeded Soviet TASS in 1992); utilizes widespread international bureaus and multiple language cycles to distribute global and regional intelligence.

  • Reuters: A prominent financial market data provider and international news service focusing on global economic data, share prices, and currency analytics alongside world reporting.

  • United Press International (UPI): Founded in 1907 by E.W. Scripps; expanded globally in 1958 via INS absorption, but suffered financial decline until acquisition by News World Communications.

  • Pan-African News Agency (PANA): Established on May 25, 1983, as an intergovernmental regional organization by African states to liberate the continent from European information colonization, correct distorted Western reportage, and promote African development.

Module 1: Foundations of Critical Writing & Thinking

Unit 1: Meaning of Critical Writing

  • Definition of Criticism: An appraisal, evaluation, or judgment offered on a piece of art, performance, or production, which may be positive, negative, or both. It requires a deeper knowledge of the art and an understanding of standards of measurement. C.T. Winchester defines it as "the intelligent appreciation of any work of art, and by consequence, the just estimate of its value and rank".

  • Four Basic Meanings of Criticism (Pope, 2002: 43):

    1. Finding fault and pulling to pieces in a negative sense.

    2. Analyzing and pulling to pieces in the neutral sense of taking apart.

    3. Interpreting with a view to establishing meaning and understanding.

    4. Evaluating with a view to establishing relative or absolute worth.

  • What it Means to be Critical: At the university level, being critical does not mean finding fault negatively; rather, it means questioning information and opinions, examining subjects from multiple perspectives, and applying theoretical frameworks. It involves appreciation (aesthetic perception), analysis (breaking content into components to see interrelations), and evaluation (rating a work based on defined criteria and purpose).

Unit 2: Critical Writing and Critical Thinking

  • Definition of Critical Thinking: The intellectually disciplined process of actively and skillfully conceptualizing, applying, analyzing, synthesizing, and/or evaluating information gathered from observation, experience, reflection, reasoning, or communication. Dewey (1933) describes it as active, persistent, and careful consideration of any belief or supposed knowledge in light of its supporting grounds.

  • Core Skills Shared by Critical Thinking and Critical Writing (Cottrell, 2005; Allen, 2004):

    1. Analysis: Breaking material into component parts to examine structure, relationships, and categories.

    2. Synthesis: Weaving together material from multiple sources and prior knowledge to create a coherent new whole.

    3. Interpretation: Comprehending and expressing the meaning, significance, or implications of data and experiences.

    4. Evaluation: Assessing the credibility of statements and the logical strength of arguments.

    5. Inference: Identifying implicit elements, drawing reasonable conclusions, and forming hypotheses.

    6. Explanation: Stating and justifying reasoning in terms of evidence, concepts, and criteria through cogent arguments.

    7. Self-Regulation: Applying analytical and evaluative skills to one's own judgments to validate or correct reasoning.

  • Mental Equipment Required by a Critic (Wolseley, 1973): A measure of objectivity, realization of the climate of opinion, awareness of methods of estimation, and perspective gained through knowledge of the art and understanding of the audience.

Unit 3: Critical Writing and Other Forms of Writing

  • Critical Writing vs. Broadcast Commentary: Both express opinions and seek to influence, but critical writing specifically evaluates what makes a work of art unique using established standards, whereas commentaries point out anomalies, explain processes, or tell stories without necessarily engaging in artistic appreciation.

  • Critical Writing vs. Editorials: Editorials represent the corporate voice of a medium on public issues to inform, explain, and persuade. Critical writing does this for artistic works while also offering entertainment and guidance to consumers.

  • Critical Writing vs. News Writing: Traditional news is a straightforward, objective, and timely recounting of facts devoid of personal opinion. Critical writing concentrates on the results of an artist's efforts and emphasizes subjective evaluation.

  • Critical Writing vs. Feature Writing: Features are creative journalistic articles focusing on human interest, personality sketches, and background stories about artists, but they do not ordinarily evaluate artistic worth.

  • Critical Writing vs. Column Writing: Columns express the regular opinions of a specialist or columnist on diverse matters. A critic frequently maintains a specialized review column, thus sharing direct overlap with columnists.

Module 2: Functions, Theories, and Approaches to Criticism

Unit 1: Functions of Criticism

  • The Function of Information: Holding up a mirror to what has been composed or performed, reporting accurately on artistic creations (Oscar Thompson in Wolseley, 1959).

  • The Function of Influence: Acting as a guide by telling consumers what a work is about, offering an expert opinion, and determining if it is worth their time and money.

  • The Function of Promotion: Serving as a publicity tool to develop a favorable public attitude toward the work of art.

  • The Function of Advertising: Attracting commercial advertising accounts and generating direct revenue for the media house, though this can sometimes create ethical tensions.

  • The Function of Prestige: Lending intellectual and cultural distinction to a publication or broadcast platform among elite audiences.

Unit 2: Theories of Criticism

  1. The Authoritarian Theory: Based on the belief that fixed, objective standards exist for judging works of art. Authoritarian critics judge new works against historical, moral, judicial, classical, and scientific models from the past. They are rigorous, disciplined, and demand strict conformity to established rules of excellence.

  2. The Impressionistic Theory: Rejects fixed rules and standards in favor of the critic's personal subjective experience. Following Anatole France's definition, the impressionistic critic "narrates the adventures of his soul among masterpieces". It evaluates a work based on its unique effect upon the critic's sensibility rather than historical conformity.

Unit 3: Approaches to Criticism (Scott, 1977)

  1. Moral Approach: Utilizes ethical principles of human conduct (order, restraint, discipline) to evaluate how a work reflects or instructs human life.

  2. Psychological Approach: Employs psychological frameworks (such as psychoanalysis) and examines the author's life and mental motivations to explain characters and creative acts.

  3. Sociological Approach: Presupposes the deep interrelatedness of art and society, evaluating a work within its historical, economic, and social context.

  4. Formalistic Approach (New Criticism): Focuses on a close, rigorous textual or aesthetic study of the work itself, independent of external social, political, or biographical ideas.

  5. Archetypal (Mythological/Ritualistic) Approach: Examines basic cultural patterns, myths, and universal symbols from which a work draws deep human resonance and appeal.

Module 3: Components of Criticism

Unit 1: Components of Criticism: The Critic (The Human Factor)

  • The Critic's Background: Encompasses formal training, broad knowledge of art history, philosophy, sociology, acquired tastes, language mastery, and an analytical mind. Building this background requires dedication and extensive exposure to standards of excellence.

  • The Critic's Attitude: Requires emotional stability, self-awareness of moods, and strict detachment from personal prejudices (such as marital quarrels or bad days). A critic must maintain open-mindedness—willingness to understand the obscure or off-beat—without lapsing into vacuity or dogmatism.

Unit 2: Components of Criticism: Direct Data (The Work Itself)

Adapted from Oscar Thompson's framework, direct data represents everything physically and intrinsically present in the work of art:

  1. Material: The physical medium itself (the text of a book, wood, canvas, pigments, stone, or theatrical costumes).

  2. Form: The structural category or label (e.g., tragedy, comedy, symphony, poetry, prose, biography) and how its structural parts interrelate.

  3. Workmanship: The craftsmanship, technical skill, originality, inspiration, and conviction with which the artist executes the material.

Unit 3: Components of Criticism: Indirect Data (Extrinsic Factors)

Indirect data stems entirely from outside the work itself:

  • The Artist's Intention: What the creator stated, disavowed, or implied about their goals.

  • The Artist's Output: The position of the work within the creator's broader career trajectory.

  • Historical & Contemporary Setting: The socio-historical significance of the work and how it compares to contemporary creations.

  • Caution: While helpful for context, indirect data must not override the intrinsic merit of the work itself.

Module 4: the Craft of Writing Reviews and Critical Articles

Unit 1: Writing a Review

  • Nature of a Review: A short piece of critical writing for newspapers or magazines about a specific art event. It blends objective news reporting with subjective evaluation.

  • Structural Formula for Beginners:

    • The Lead: Captures the 5 Ws and H (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How) using an engaging summary or news-peg.

    • The Body: Balances reporting with critical analysis in decreasing order of importance, integrating direct and indirect quotes.

    • The Ending: Concludes with a wrap-up evaluation, a final major point, or a clinching summary.

  • Copy Preparation Rules: Take extensive notes, respect publication length limits (300–600 words), use third-person pronouns to maintain objective authority, select precise vocabulary, and avoid libellous personal attacks on artists.

Unit 2: Writing a Critical Article

  • Nature of a Critical Article: A more extensive, intensive, and penetrating prose composition than a review. It evaluates an entire body of work, a complete artistic career, or a broad field. It requires the writer to be idea-minded rather than merely thing-minded.

  • Seven Steps in Writing a Critical Article:

    1. Receive or conceptualize a clear topic/idea.

    2. Conduct thorough research via observation, interviews, and literature.

    3. Analyze and reflect upon findings to discover new insights.

    4. Develop an organized outline grouping related points.

    5. Craft an engaging opening (news-peg or anecdote) and a thematic conclusion.

    6. Present arguments using the deductive method (general to particular) or inductive method (particular to general).

    7. Draft, revise, edit, and polish the copy.

Unit 3: The Reviewer and the Critic

  • The Reviewer: Operates primarily as an objective reporter covering a timely art event. Their virtues are speed, topicality, wit, and factual accuracy, helping readers decide whether to attend or buy.

  • The Critic: Operates as a philosophical analyst who publishes after deeper meditation. Their virtues are profound insight, a firm historical sense, and absolute truth, aiming to shape reader tastes and long-term artistic understanding.

Unit 4: The Critic's Style

  • Journalistic Style Requirements: Conforms to press traditions using short sentences, simple words, brief paragraphs, and avoidance of unnecessary jargon, while retaining freedom to use specialized artistic terminology.

  • Tone and Cultivation: Must avoid pomposity, feigned radicalism, excessive wisecracks, and trite clichés. Good style shows no sign of effort; it is achieved through extensive reading, lifelong writing practice, and honest self-expression.

Module 5: Reviewing Specific Art Genres

Unit 1: Reviewing the Fine Arts (Painting and Sculpture)

  • Challenges: Painting and sculpture are highly subjective visual arts lacking fixed verbal texts. Reviewers must evaluate color quality, pigments, brushwork, framing, lighting, presentation, and artistic philosophy.

  • Methodology: Apply the material-form-workmanship formula, investigate the artist's originality against historical precedents, and report on exhibition logistics (galleries, dates, hours) using a straight news framework.

Unit 2: Reviewing Books

  • Methodology: Maintain an active reading notebook, record page references for striking passages, and classify the book (biography, fiction, history).

  • Historical/General Criteria Questions:

    1. Does it establish factual details through strict historical method?

    2. Does it possess a philosophy or frame of reference of more than transient significance?

    3. Is it written in a clear style?

    4. Does it present new data or interpretations rather than mere hackwork?

    5. Does the author address universal human questions?

Unit 3: Reviewing Stage Play or Drama

  • Methodology: Assess whether the script is new, provide a brief synopsis and early verdict, evaluate plot universality, characterization (true-to-life vs. caricature), dramatic pacing, stage direction, and technical credits (lighting, costume, set design). Balance personal opinions with fair credit to producers, directors, and actors.

Unit 4: Reviewing Music

  • Methodology: Account for the ephemeral and subjective nature of music across genres (pop, classical, highlife, jazz, reggae). Evaluate technical perfection, sound balancing, mixing, acoustic presence, recording quality (live concert vs. studio stereo), and interpretative fidelity compared to previous recordings.

Unit 5: Reviewing Television and Motion Pictures

  • Motion Pictures: Evaluate two-dimensional camera work, mobile framing, special effects (substance vs. gimmick), plot integrity, and characterization within cinematic conventions.

  • Television Programs: Evaluate adherence to strict time limits, appropriateness of broadcast time slots, commercial placement disruptions, audio volume consistency, program cycles (situation comedies, documentaries), and target audience satisfaction.

Module 1: Photography As a Profession

Unit 1: History and Definition of Photography

  • What is Photography?

    • Derived from Greek words photos (light) and graphein (to draw).

    • Spencer (1973) defines it as the art, science, and practice of creating durable images by recording light or other electromagnetic radiation, either chemically via light-sensitive film or electronically via an image sensor.

    • Collins English Dictionary defines it as the art, practice, or occupation of taking and printing photographs and making cine films.

  • Types of Photographs (Based on Subject Awareness):

    1. The Aware Photograph: Arranged and planned; the subject is fully conscious that their picture is being taken.

    2. The Semi-Aware Photograph: The subject is partially aware or adjusts posture in the presence of the photographer, though the exact moment of the click is unknown.

    3. The Candid Photograph: Factual, unarranged, capturing life and action as it unfolds naturally. Requires speed, instinct, and anticipation.

  • History of Photography:

    • Roots in antiquity with the camera obscura principle. Mo Ti (5th Century BC China) and Greek philosophers Aristotle and Euclid observed pinhole image inversion.

    • Hercules Florence (1832) studied permanent pinhole image fixing, naming it "photographia," but failed to publish widely. Sir John F.W. Herschel coined the word "photography" in 1839.

    • Joseph Nicéphore Niépce produced the oldest surviving permanent photograph (Heliograph / sun print) on a polished plate in 1827.

    • Louis Daguerre partnered with Niépce and invented the daguerreotype in 1839, fixing images on silver-plated copper sheets soaked in iodine.

Unit 2: Photography: A Profession or Craft

  • Why Photography Matters in Human Life:

    1. Part of our Legacy: Freezes fleeting moments that outlive us, serving as visual pieces of our personal and societal history.

    2. Sharing and Communication: Fulfills the human desire to share beauty, experiences, and perspectives with strangers and loved ones globally.

    3. Expression of Art: Allows creators to express emotional and aesthetic viewpoints through visual mediums.

    4. Complex Language: Provides a visual vocabulary to convey complex human emotions, joy, sorrow, and psychological states when words fail.

    5. Power to Move Us: Captures attention instantly and speaks directly to human emotions and empathy.

  • Reasons for Studying Photography:

    • Employment Opportunities: Continuous demand for professional photographers across diverse industries.

    • Overcoming Boredom: Fieldwork prevents individuals from being confined to standard desk jobs.

    • Financial Flexibility: Lucrative career paths with freelancing options allowing self-managed rates and hours.

    • Creativity: Offers infinite outlets for artistic self-expression.

  • Photography in Nigeria: Traced through archives like the Eliot Elisofon Photographic Archives, early postcards, and pioneer local photographers such as H. Sanya Freeman (Photographer-in-Chief to the Governor of Nigeria circa 1910), Neils Walwin Holm, and Solomon Osagie Alonge.

Unit 3: Professional Photographers

  • Who is a Professional Photographer? An individual who engages in photography as a primary source of income and livelihood. They may be full-time newspaper/magazine employees, contracted event coverage specialists, or independent freelancers (including paparazzi and fine art photographers).

  • Copyright and Legal Protection in Photography: The author of a photographic negative/file owns the copyright. Commercial users purchase licenses or usage rights via contracts specifying territory, duration, and frequency, distinct from production fees.

Unit 4: Types and Forms of Pictures in Mass Media

  • Functions of Pictures in Mass Media:

    1. Credibility: Makes events seem real and true, lending undeniable authenticity to news reports.

    2. Visual Appeal: Acts as visual magnets and entry points, breaking layout monotony with contrasting tones.

    3. Vitality: Adds life and human-interest energy to stories in ways words cannot match.

    4. Aesthetics: Introduces beauty and artistic appeal to publication pages.

  • News Photojournalism: The art of reporting news in pictures, divided into hard news photographs (disasters, wars, major government events) and soft news photographs (ceremonies, foundation layings, seminars).

Module 2: the Core of Photojournalism

Unit 1: A Professional Photo-Journalist

  • Distinction between Photographer and Photojournalist: While traditional photographers capture static "nouns" (people, places, things), photojournalists hunt and capture active "verbs" (explodes, cries, kicks, protests) that tell a developing story.

  • Types of Photojournalists:

    1. Commercial Photojournalist: Specialized in advertising; operates under intense pressure to produce creative studio setups, props, and still-life shots for brands.

    2. Fashion Photojournalist: Focuses on style, clothing, accessories, and models, requiring interpersonal tact to guide and relax subjects.

    3. Industrial Photojournalist: Employed in-plant or freelance by corporations to cover AGMs, factory operations, industrial manuals, and staff events.

    4. Freelance Photojournalist: Offers independent services for hire across diverse market sectors.

    5. Press Photojournalist: Possesses a sharp "news nose," operating with absolute speed to capture breaking events before print deadlines.

  • Qualities and Attributes of a Professional Photojournalist: Possesses artistic and technical skills, patience, high concentration, emotional stability, ability to interpret editors' ideas, strict adherence to ethics, and respect for legal boundaries.

Unit 2: The Place of Photography in Journalism

  • Visual reporting acts as an indispensable partner to text, bridging literacy gaps and providing multi-coded memory traces that enhance information recall and emotional engagement among readers.

Module 3: Photo Editing and Publication

Unit 1: Photo Editing: Meaning and Origin

  • Definition: Photo editing is the process of selecting, cropping, adjusting, and refining crude photographic shots to make them fit for publication, ensure clarity, uphold moral values, and protect against legal liabilities.

  • Reasons for Photo Editing:

    1. Clarity: Correcting blurriness, poor lighting, low contrast, or submerged backgrounds caused by camera limitations or poor environmental angles.

    2. Specific Targets: Focusing attention on relevant details while masking distracting background elements.

    3. Moral Value: Eliminating obscene, indecent, or dehumanizing elements that violate societal standards.

    4. Beautification: Enhancing aesthetic presentation and correcting technical defects like "red-eye".

    5. Legal Implications: Blurring faces of unconvicted suspects or editing backgrounds to prevent defamatory associations (libel).

Unit 2: Editing Concepts in Photo-Journalism (1)

  • Captioning (Cutline): A concise descriptive block accompanying an image that answers the 5 Ws (Who, What, When, Where, Why/How). Good captions avoid trite phrases ("looks on"), refrain from editorializing or guessing emotions, reflect the exact image, and maintain strict spelling accuracy.

  • Blurring: A technique where parts of an image are faded or covered to protect personal dignity, privacy, moral values, or legal compliance.

Unit 3: Editing Concepts in Photo-Journalism (2)

  • Scaling, Cutting (Cropping), and Fixing: Techniques used to resize photographs to fit specific page layout dimensions. Methods include Electronic Terminals, Proportion Wheels, the Ratio Method ($\text{RW} \div \text{OW} \times \text{OD} = \text{RD}$), and the Diagonal Method. Cropping removes unwanted details to improve composition.

  • Bleeding: Running photographs intentionally off one or more trim edges of a page with no surrounding margin.

  • Flopping: Mirror-reversing an image across a vertical axis to improve aesthetic appeal, adapt to right/left-hand drive markets, direct a subject's gaze into text columns, or respect cultural taboos (e.g., avoiding left-hand eating depictions in Muslim societies).

  • Retouching: Correcting physical blemishes, red-eye, and smoothing skin tones in portrait images.

Unit 4: Computer and Adobe Photo Shop

  • Photoshopping: Using digital software programs (like Adobe Photoshop) to edit images. While legitimate for technical enhancement, unethical manipulation creates visual parodies or digital fakery intended to deceive.

  • Microsoft Office Picture Manager: A basic image management tool supporting cropping, resizing, color fine-tuning, red-eye removal, and batch renaming.

Unit 1: Camera types and parts

  • Camera Types:

    • Point and Shoot (P&S): Automatic focus, fixed/built-in features, lightweight, ideal for casual snapshots.

    • Compact/Digital Cameras: Versatile range from camera phones to ultra-compact pocket devices and bridge cameras.

  • Key Camera Parts & Uses:

    • Body: The foundational housing enclosing the film/sensor, battery, and internal controls.

    • Lens: Focuses incoming light onto the film or digital sensor via adjustable apertures.

    • Viewfinder: The sighting window used by the photographer to frame and compose shots (TTL viewfinders use mirrors for absolute accuracy).

    • Shutter Release: The button that activates the mechanical/electronic shutter to expose the sensor to light.

    • Shutter / Shutter Speed Control: Controls the duration light reaches the sensor, measured in fractions of a second (e.g., $1/60$ s).

    • Film Speed Control (ISO): Calibrates the camera meter to light sensitivity.

    • F-Stop Control: Regulates the aperture size within the lens.

    • Hot Shoe Mount: The electrical contact bracket on top of SLR cameras for attaching external flashes.

  • Camera Supports: Tripods, monopods, rifle grips, bean bags, A-clamps, and magnetic supports (magnapols) used to eliminate camera shake.

  • Camera Techniques: Utilizing revealing angles, maintaining clear focus, keeping subjects within frame, and avoiding visual obstructions.

Unit 2: Professional Photo Studio

  • Studio Setup Requirements: A dedicated, windowless room with concrete white-painted flooring; 3 umbrella continuous lights; 3 umbrella strobe lights; top-mounted and ring flashes; stable storage stages/crates; adjustable metal trusses; ladders; color gels; light meters; White Balance Cards (WBC); remote shutter releases; cooling fans; and surge-protected power strips.

Module 5: Photojournalism Law and Ethics

Unit 1: Ethics of Photojournalism

  • Core Ethical Principles (NPPA & Course Standards):

    1. Accuracy: Maintain factual records of time, date, and circumstances; never stage-manage or artificially manipulate shoots; avoid editing beyond technical necessity.

    2. Impartiality: Remain objective, avoid personal bias, and represent all sides of a story fairly.

    3. Taste, Decency, & Offence: Avoid gratuitous shock imagery that exploits victims or sensationalizes tragedy.

    4. Privacy & Consent: Respect personal privacy, especially concerning vulnerable subjects and victims of grief.

    5. Integrity of the Subject: Never expose subjects to ridicule, humiliation, or undue physical danger.

    6. Professional Dignity: Refuse bribes, gifts, or financial favours in exchange for biased coverage.

Unit 2: Photojournalism and the Law

  • Copyright Law: Protects original photographic works, vesting ownership in the creator (or employing media organization), prohibiting unauthorized reproduction.

  • Obscenity: Publishing materials that tend to deprave, corrupt, or violate societal moral standards is a punishable offence.

  • Sedition: Publishing photographs intended to incite hatred, disaffection, or violent revolt against government officials or promote hostility between population classes.

  • Invasion of Privacy: Protects individuals against unauthorized intrusion, publication of embarrassing private facts, misappropriation of likeness, and false light distortion.

  • The Advertising Tripod Model: The structure of advertising relies on three foundational, interdependent legs known as the Advertising Tripod: The Advertiser (Client), The Advertising Agency, and The Media.

  • The Advertiser: This is the client, manufacturer, or service provider who conceives the product, funds the marketing budget, and provides the initial marketing brief outlining what needs to be communicated.

  • The Advertising Agency: This is the professional, creative organization hired by the advertiser to research the market, formulate the creative strategy, design the messages, and manage the execution of the campaign.

  • The Media: These are the communication channels (print, broadcast, outdoor, digital) that serve as vehicles to transport the commercial message from the creator to the target audience.

  • Collaborative Relationship: Just like a physical tripod, if any of the three legs is missing, weak, or misaligned, the entire structure collapses. The advertiser provides the resources and product vision; the agency translates this into persuasive creative art and strategy; and the media ensures optimal audience delivery. Their relationship must be highly symbiotic and coordinated for the advertising programme to succeed.

b. Justify why skipping formal planning results in resource misapplication and campaign failure in contemporary Nigerian advertising practice.

  • Prevents Resource Wastage: Formal planning ensures that financial resources, time, and creative energy are channeled strictly toward defined target audiences rather than scattered haphazardly across unproductive media.

  • Provides a Strategic Roadmap: Planning acts as a 'guide' and 'guardian', establishing clear benchmarks, objectives, and execution timelines so all agency departments operate with synchronized focus.

  • Mitigates Market Risk: In a competitive and volatile economy like Nigeria's, consumer preferences shift rapidly. Structured planning involves thorough market research and situation audits, shielding brands from costly consumer rejection and regulatory infractions.

  • Measures Return on Investment (ROI): Planning establishes clear evaluation criteria, enabling advertisers to track whether campaign goals (awareness, trial, loyalty) were successfully achieved.

Module 1: Organizational Structure & Departmentalization

Q1. Discuss the primary departments in a typical broadcast media station and their functions.

Departmentalization ensures division of labor, operational efficiency, clear lines of authority, and accountability:

  1. Administration / Human Resources Department:

    • Function: Handles staff recruitment, orientation, training, staff welfare, industrial relations, record-keeping, and legal compliance.

  2. Programmes Department:

    • Function: Responsible for creating, acquiring, planning, scheduling, and executing all non-news audio/visual content (talk shows, dramas, documentaries, music blocks).

  3. News and Current Affairs Department:

    • Function: Gathers, writes, edits, investigates, and broadcasts news bulletins, commentaries, and special investigative reports.

  4. Engineering / Technical Services Department:

    • Function: Maintains, repairs, and operates broadcast transmitters, studio mixing consoles, cameras, antennas, link systems, and power backup units to guarantee uninterrupted transmission.

  5. Commercial / Marketing / Sales Department:

    • Function: Generates revenue by selling commercial airtime, spot announcements, program sponsorships, and event coverage to advertisers and agencies.

  6. Finance and Accounts Department:

    • Function: Manages financial planning, payroll preparation, revenue collection, auditing, station budgeting, and expenditure tracking.

  7. Production Department:

    • Function: Coordinates technical production execution, studio setup, camera directing, audio mixing, lighting, and post-production editing for studio and field recordings.

  8. Traffic / Operations Department:

    • Function: Bridges marketing and programming by managing the broadcast log—scheduling commercial spots, ensuring sponsors' ads run in their paid slots, and logging transmitted airtime.

  9. Public Relations / Corporate Communications Department:

    • Function: Manages the station's public image, community outreach programs, viewer/listener complaints, press releases, and corporate brand reputation.

Module 2: Human Resource & Personnel Management

Q2. Functions and services performed by the Personnel/Human Resources Department.

  1. Recruitment and Selection: Sourcing qualified broadcast professionals (reporters, engineers, presenters) matching job descriptions.

  2. Employee Training and Development: Organizing continuous capacity-building workshops on digital media software, broadcast ethics, and technical operations.

Q3. Theory X / Theory Y and Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs in Media Management.

  • Douglas McGregor’s Theory X & Theory Y:

    • Theory X Postulations (Authoritarian):

      1. The average employee inherently dislikes work and will avoid it if possible.

      2. Employees must be coerced, controlled, directed, or threatened with punishment to achieve station goals.

      3. The average worker prefers to be directed, avoids responsibility, and seeks security above all.

      4. Workers lack ambition and resist organizational change.

      5. Staff require close, constant supervision in daily operations.

    • Theory Y Postulations (Participative):

      1. Expending physical and mental effort in work is as natural as play or rest.

      2. Employees exercise self-direction and self-control in serving objectives to which they are committed.

      3. Commitment to objectives is a function of rewards associated with their achievement.

      4. The average worker learns, under proper conditions, to accept and seek responsibility.

      5. Creative problem-solving capacity is widely distributed across the staff.

  • Abraham Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs (Media Context):

    1. Physiological Needs: Basic salary, comfortable studio environment, adequate air-conditioning for equipment and human comfort.

    2. Safety Needs: Job security, prompt salary payments, physical safety equipment for field journalists covering crisis zones.

    3. Social / Belongingness Needs: Team spirit in newsroom and production crews, healthy workplace culture, social gatherings.

    4. Esteem Needs: Public recognition (bylines, awards), promotions, title advancements (e.g., Senior Editor).

    5. Self-Actualization: Creative freedom to produce groundbreaking investigative documentaries or pioneer innovative station formats.

Module 3: Management Functions, Skills & Theoretical Schools

Q4. Core Functions of Management in a Broadcast Station.

  1. Planning: Setting station goals, audience targets, revenue benchmarks, and formulating short/long-term programming schedules.

  2. Organizing: Structuring departments, allocating studio time, assigning beats to reporters, and deploying equipment.

  3. Directing / Leading: Guiding, motivating, and supervising staff daily to maintain production deadlines and editorial quality.

  4. Staffing: Recruiting, deploying, training, and placing the right talent in appropriate roles across departments.

  5. Controlling / Evaluating: Monitoring broadcast quality, auditing accounts, checking regulatory compliance (NBC Code), and comparing results against targets.

Q5. Essential Management Skills for a Station General Manager.

  1. Technical Skill: Practical understanding of broadcast technology, digital consoles, transmitters, and production workflows.

  2. Human / Interpersonal Skill: Ability to motivate creative, temperamental talent, manage team dynamics, and resolve workplace conflicts.

  3. Conceptual Skill: Ability to see the station as an integrated whole within the broader media industry and formulate long-term strategic visions.

  4. Financial Management Skill: Competence in reading balance sheets, managing cash flow, preparing budgets, and driving profitability.

  5. Political / External Relations Skill: Ability to navigate regulatory bodies (e.g., National Broadcasting Commission), handle government relations, and maintain strong community ties.

Q6. Theoretical Approaches to Management.

  • Classical School: Focuses on high efficiency, formal structure, and clear authority lines.

    • Scientific Management (Frederick Taylor): Emphasizes time-and-motion studies, task optimization, scientific selection of workers, and financial piece-rate incentives.

    • Administrative Management (Henri Fayol): Focuses on organizational principles: Unity of Command, Division of Labor, Scalar Chain, Esprit de Corps, and 14 principles of management.

    • Bureaucratic Management (Max Weber): Stresses formal rules, impersonal relationships, clear hierarchy, written record-keeping, and merit-based promotion.

  • Human Relations School (Elton Mayo - Hawthorne Studies): Asserts that social factors, psychological needs, informal group dynamics, worker satisfaction, and employee participation drive productivity more than physical conditions or monetary incentives.

  • Modern Approaches: Include Systems Theory (viewing the station as an open system interacting with its external environment) and Contingency Theory (asserting there is no "one best way" to manage; optimal leadership depends on the specific situation).

Module 5: Broadcast Studio Equipment & Transmission Infrastructure

Q9. Radio Studio Definition, Equipment, and Floor Characteristics.

  • Definition: A sound-proofed, acoustically treated room designed specifically for the origination, production, recording, and broadcasting of audio content without external noise interference.

  • Seven Essential Radio Studio Equipment Items:

    1. Audio Mixing Console (Sound Board): Combines, routes, processes, and adjusts volume levels of multiple incoming audio signals (microphones, music players, phone lines).

    2. Microphones (Condenser/Dynamic): Converts sound waves from voices into electrical signals for transmission/recording.

    3. Studio Monitors (Speakers) & Headphones: High-fidelity playback speakers for listening to mix output; headphones allow presenters/guests to hear themselves and incoming cues without causing audio feedback loops.

    4. Playout Automation Computer System: Stores digital music libraries, commercials, jingles, station IDs, and automates program logs.

    5. Audio Processors & Equalizers: Cleans, compresses, and balances audio frequencies to deliver a uniform, crisp broadcast signal.

    6. Telephone Hybrid: Connects phone lines directly to the audio console, allowing callers to speak on-air with acoustic echo cancellation.

    7. Acoustic Treatment Panels & "On-Air" Light: Wall treatments preventing sound reflections/reverberation; illuminated light sign outside alerting people that live recording is active.

  • Characteristics of a Studio Floor: Must be level, non-reflective, sound-dampened (carpeted or specialized rubberized acoustic floor), insulated against structural vibration, and clean.

Q10. Three Essential Components of Radio Transmission.

  1. Transmitter: Generates and amplifies the Radio Frequency (RF) carrier signal, modulating it with the audio signal received from the studio mixer.

  2. Transmission Line (Coaxial Cable / Waveguide): The heavy-duty shielded cable that carries the high-power RF signal from the transmitter to the antenna atop the mast.

  3. Antenna (Radiating System): Radiates the RF electrical energy out into space as electromagnetic waves for reception by radio receivers.

Module 6: Types of Radio Broadcasting & Digital Convergence

Q11. Seven Types of Radio Broadcasting & Characteristics.

  1. Public Service Radio: State/government-funded (e.g., FRCN); mandated to inform, educate, and serve the public interest across all demographics without commercial bias.

  2. Commercial Radio: Privately owned, profit-driven stations funded entirely by advertising revenue and sponsorships; highly formatted to maximize ratings.

  3. Community Radio: Non-profit, locally owned station serving a specific geographic community or interest group; operated by community members to address local issues.

  4. Campus / Educational Radio: Operated by tertiary institutions (e.g., UNILAG 103.1 FM, NOUN FM) primarily for training mass communication students and serving campus news.

  5. Satellite Radio: Subscription-based digital radio transmitted directly from geostationary satellites to special receivers over massive geographical distances (e.g., SiriusXM).

  6. HD (High-Definition) / Digital Radio: Transmits digital audio signals alongside traditional analog signals over standard FM/AM frequencies, delivering CD-quality sound and data text.

  7. Internet / Webcast Radio: Streams audio content over IP networks (websites/mobile apps) globally, bypassing traditional frequency licenses and geographic boundaries.

Q12. Five Points of Digital Convergence & Managerial Implications.

  1. Content / Production Convergence: Multi-skilling required—journalists write text, record audio, and shoot video simultaneously.

  2. Distribution / Transmission Convergence: Broadcasting simultaneously over FM airwaves, satellite, mobile apps, and social media live streams.

  3. Device / Receiver Convergence: Audiences consume radio content on smartphones, smart TVs, and connected car dashboards rather than standalone radio boxes.

  4. Interactive / Audience Convergence: Shift from one-way broadcasting to real-time audience feedback via WhatsApp, social media comments, and live polls.

  5. Economic / Business Model Convergence: Revenue streams shift from purely traditional airtime spots to digital ad banners, podcast sponsorships, and paid online subscriptions.

  • Managerial Implications: Station managers must invest in staff digital retraining, upgrade technical IP infrastructure, adopt multi-platform monetization, and continuously monitor social media analytics.

Q18. Five Common Broadcast Programme Scheduling Strategies.

  1. Block Programming: Scheduling a series of similar-genre shows back-to-back (e.g., two hours of sitcoms or three hours of news) to retain a specific demographic block.

  2. Stripping: Scheduling the same show at the exact same time slot every day from Monday to Friday (e.g., running a soap opera at 7:00 PM daily).

  3. Hammocking: Placing a new or weak show between two established, highly popular hit shows to boost its viewership via audience flow.

  4. Tentpoling: Placing a strong flagship hit show in the middle of a prime-time block to support weaker shows before and after it.

  5. Counter-Programming: Airing content completely different from a competitor's top show at the exact same time slot (e.g., airing live sports against a rival station's soap opera).

Q19. Explanatory Notes on Media Management Concepts.

  • Pay for Play ("Payola"): Illegal or unethical practice of accepting money or gifts from record labels/artists to play specific songs on air without disclosing the sponsorship to listeners.

  • First-Run Syndication: Content produced specifically to be sold directly to independent local stations for their first airing, rather than originally created for a major network network feed.

  • Vertical Integration: Business strategy where a media company owns multiple stages of the supply chain—from production studios and distribution networks down to retail broadcast stations.

  • Broadband Distribution: High-speed internet delivery networks (fiber-optic, 4G/5G) used to stream high-definition broadcast audio and video content directly to connected end-user devices.

Detailed Course Material Solutions

Module 1: Foundations of Communication & Process

Q1. Definitions & Relationship

  • Communication: Derived from the Latin communis (common). It is a two-way dynamic process of sharing, exchanging, or transmitting information, ideas, attitudes, or feelings between a sender and a receiver through a mutually understandable channel/code.

  • Development: A multi-dimensional, continuous process involving the qualitative and quantitative transformation of a society’s economic, social, political, and cultural structures to improve the well-being and quality of life of its citizens.

  • Relationship: Communication is the engine and catalyst of development. Development cannot occur in a vacuum; communication raises awareness, shares innovations, facilitates dialogue, mobilizes communities, and empowers people to participate in decisions affecting their lives.

Q2. Communication Elements & Process

Communication is a dynamic system operating through interrelated elements in sequential order:

  1. Source / Sender: The originator of the message who encodes the idea.

  2. Encoding: Translating thoughts/ideas into symbolic forms (words, visuals, gestures).

  3. Message: The physical or verbal product containing the information/idea.

  4. Channel / Medium: The pathway through which the message travels (mass media, interpersonal, folk media).

  5. Receiver: The intended audience who decodes the message.

  6. Decoding: Translating symbols back into understandable thoughts.

  7. Feedback: The receiver's response sent back to the sender, turning the process into a two-way loop.

  8. Noise: Interference (physical, psychological, or semantic) that distorts the message.

Osgood & Schramm Model: Highlights communication as a circular process where both parties act as encoder, interpreter, and decoder. The message is the central element embedded in shared "fields of experience."

Q3. Levels of Communication

  • Intrapersonal: Communication within oneself (thinking, decision-making).

  • Interpersonal: Direct, face-to-face exchange between two or three people.

  • Group Communication: Interaction among members of a small group (e.g., village meeting).

  • Mass Communication: Mediated transmission of messages to large, heterogeneous, widely dispersed audiences via mass media.

  • Organizational Communication: Internal and external communication within structured institutions.

Q4. Monologic vs. Dialogic Communication

  • Monologic Mode: One-way, top-down transmission of messages. The communicator acts as an expert imparting knowledge to a passive audience (associated with the traditional/dominant paradigm).

  • Dialogic Mode: Two-way, horizontal, interactive communication based on mutual exchange, horizontal dialogue, active listener participation, and shared meaning (associated with participatory development).

Module 2: Concepts & Appellations of Development Communication

Q1. Definition & Appellations

Development Communication (DevComm) is the integration of strategic communication processes, tools, and techniques into development policies and projects to facilitate dialogue, social change, and sustainable development.

Appellations (Alternative Names/Labels):

  1. Development Support Communication (DSC): Focuses specifically on using communication to support concrete, project-based development goals.

  2. Communication for Development (C4D): Term heavily utilized by UN agencies (UNICEF, UNESCO) placing beneficiaries at the center of social change.

  3. Programme Communication: Communication strategies designed for specific intervention programmes (e.g., immunization campaigns).

  4. Social Change Communication: Emphasizes shifting social norms, beliefs, and structural behaviors.

  5. Participatory Communication: Highlights bottom-up community empowerment and ownership.

Q2. Mefalopulos' (2008) 10 Key Facts (Select 5)

  1. DevComm is about dialogue, not top-down dissemination.

  2. DevComm addresses behavioral and social change, not just information sharing.

  3. DevComm must be integrated from the planning stage, not added as an afterthought.

  4. DevComm uses multi-channel approaches matching target audience habits.

  5. DevComm empowers communities through active participation and ownership.

Module 3: Roles of Communication & Mass Media in Development

Q1. Roles of Mass Media in Development (Okigbo, 1991; Soola, 2003)

  1. Information Dissemination: Creating awareness about modern practices in health, agriculture, and civic duties.

  2. Education & Skill Acquisition: Teaching rural dwellers new vocational and agricultural skills.

  3. Social Mobilization: Galvanizing people to take collective action (e.g., sanitation, vaccination).

  4. Agenda Setting: Focusing public attention on critical development issues (e.g., erosion, literacy).

  5. Watchdog / Accountability: Monitoring government development projects to reduce corruption.

  6. Cultural Preservation & Integration: Promoting local culture while building national unity.

Q2. Communication for Lifestyle Changes & Mobilization

Communication shifts knowledge into practice by:

  • Concretizing Abstract Ideas: Translating complex health advice into actionable steps.

  • Creating Social Norms: Demonstrating community adoption to lower peer resistance.

  • Engaging Community Leaders: Using traditional rulers to validate development campaigns.

  1. Advocacy: Securing policy commitment, political goodwill, and resources.

  2. Social Mobilization: Engaging civil society, community groups, and NGOs.

  3. Program Communication (BCC): Empowering individuals/families to adopt positive behaviors.

  4. Capacity Building: Training media practitioners, fieldworkers, and health managers.

  5. Monitoring & Evaluation: Tracking progress and assessing outcome impacts.

Module 4: Development Paradigms & Theories

Q1 & Q2. Dominant vs. Alternative Paradigm & Failure Factors

  • Dominant Paradigm: Top-down, modernization-oriented approach post-WWII. Assumed development equals economic growth/industrialization, and western media content would "trickle down" to modernize traditional societies.

  • Why it failed (5 Factors):

    1. Ignored local socio-cultural, political, and economic contexts.

    2. Treated rural dwellers as passive, ignorant receivers rather than active participants.

    3. Over-emphasized technical/capital transfers without human capacity building.

    4. Widened the gap between urban elites and rural poor.

    5. Relied on one-way mass media top-down broadcasts (monologic).

  • Alternative/Participatory Paradigm: Bottom-up, horizontal model prioritizing self-reliance, local knowledge, dialogue, community participation, and human empowerment.

Q3. 7 Reasons for Adopting Participatory Paradigm

  1. Ensures local ownership and sustainability of projects.

  2. Respects and utilizes indigenous knowledge systems.

  3. Promotes empowerment and self-reliance among marginal populations.

  4. Enhances project relevance by diagnosing actual vs. perceived needs.

  5. Reduces project failure and community resistance.

  6. Fosters democratic decision-making at the grassroots level.

  7. Promotes long-term behavioral change through active dialogue.

Q4. Theoretical Perspectives

  • Modernization Theory (Ward & Rustow / Rostow): Outlines societal growth in linear stages: (1) Traditional Society, (2) Preconditions for Take-off, (3) Take-off, (4) Drive to Maturity, (5) Age of High Mass Consumption.

  • Dependency Theory: Argues that underdevelopment in the "Periphery" (developing nations) is directly caused by economic exploitation from the "Core" (western nations).

  • Diffusion of Innovations (Rogers): Explains how new ideas spread through a social system over time.

    • 5 Attributes: Relative Advantage, Compatibility, Complexity, Trialability, Observability.

    • 5 Decision Process Stages: Knowledge $\rightarrow$ Persuasion $\rightarrow$ Decision $\rightarrow$ Implementation $\rightarrow$ Confirmation.

  • Agenda-Setting Theory: Media do not tell people what to think, but what to think about by prioritizing developmental topics.

Model answer — Question 1

(a) Identify five (5) elements of communication and explain how each directly affects the development communication process. (12.5 Marks)

Communication operates as an interrelated system. In development communication (DevComm), each element directly influences whether a message leads to awareness, understanding, or sustainable behavioral change:

  1. The Source / Sender: The originator of the message (e.g., a development officer, health worker, or government agency). In DevComm, the source's credibility, empathy, and cultural understanding determine whether rural communities accept or reject the message. If the source is perceived as an out-of-touch outsider, community resistance occurs regardless of how good the message is.

  2. The Message: The actual content, idea, or innovation being communicated (e.g., proper sanitation practices or new farming techniques). For development communication to be effective, the message must be simple, culturally appropriate, relevant, and actionable. Complex jargon or messages that clash with local religious or cultural beliefs stall the development process.

  3. The Channel / Medium: The pathway or tool used to convey the message (e.g., radio, town criers, interpersonal meetings, television). Selecting the wrong channel—such as using print leaflets in an illiterate community—blocks the development objective. The channel must align with local access, language, and media consumption habits.

  4. The Receiver: The target audience or community members whose behavior or knowledge is meant to change. The receiver's pre-existing beliefs, literacy levels, socioeconomic status, and readiness to adopt change directly dictate how the message is decoded and acted upon.

  5. Feedback: The reaction or response sent back from the receiver to the sender. In development communication, feedback transforms a one-way broadcast into a two-way dialogue. It allows project planners to identify misconceptions, evaluate progress, and refine interventions based on real community feedback.

(b) Discuss five (5) major factors responsible for the failure of the Dominant Paradigm of development communication. (12.5 Marks)

The Dominant Paradigm (popular post-World War II) equated development with industrialization, economic growth, and Westernization, relying on top-down media dissemination. Its failure was driven by five critical flaws:

  1. Top-Down, Monologic Communication: It treated rural populations as passive receivers of instructions from urban experts rather than active participants in their own development. This lack of dialogue created apathy and resentment.

  2. Neglect of Local and Cultural Contexts: It assumed Western models of industrial progress could be uniformly applied to developing nations, ignoring indigenous knowledge systems, local traditions, religious dynamics, and traditional governance structures.

  3. Over-Emphasis on Technical and Capital Inputs: It operated on the false premise that simply transferring technology, machinery, or money would automatically lead to development, completely neglecting human capacity building and social readiness.

  4. Widening Social and Economic Gaps: Information and resources disseminated under this paradigm were primarily captured by educated urban elites and large landowners, worsening inequalities between the rich and the marginalized poor.

  5. Assuming Audience Homogeneity: It viewed rural communities as monolithic groups with identical needs, ignoring important internal differences such as gender roles, age brackets, literacy levels, and socio-economic statuses.

Module 1: Foundational Concepts in Broadcasting

Q1. Define Broadcasting, Programme, Programming, Format, and Station Philosophy.

  • Broadcasting: The electronic transmission of audio or audio-visual signals through electromagnetic waves (or cable/IP networks) from a central transmitter to a large, heterogeneous, widely dispersed, and simultaneous audience using appropriate receiving apparatus.

  • Broadcast Programme: A distinct, complete unit of audio or video content produced for transmission to an audience (e.g., a news bulletin, drama episode, talk show, or documentary).

  • Broadcast Programming: The strategic, continuous process of planning, selecting, organizing, and placing broadcast programmes into a logical daily/weekly schedule or grid to attract, retain, and satisfy specific target audiences while meeting station objectives.

  • Format: The overall recognizable sound, structure, or identity of a station's broadcast output (e.g., All-News, Top 40 Music, Sports Talk). It dictates the style, tone, pacing, and mix of content aired continuously.

  • Station Philosophy: The core mission, editorial stance, values, and commercial orientation of a broadcast station that guides its content creation, news judgment, and programming choices.

Q2. Differentiate between Broadcasting, Narrowcasting, and Cablecasting with examples.

  • Broadcasting: Transmitting signals over open airwaves intended for general public reception without restriction.

    • Examples: Radio Nigeria (FRCN), NTA Network, Channels TV.

  • Narrowcasting: Transmitting content tailored to a highly specific, niche audience or localized demographic group rather than the general public.

    • Examples: Closed-circuit campus radio stations, weather-only channels, in-store radio networks.

  • Cablecasting: The distribution of radio or television signals to subscribers through coaxial or fiber-optic cables rather than over-the-air radio frequency spectrums.

    • Examples: DSTV/MultiChoice cable services, fiber IPTV home setups.

Module 2: Media Comparison: Radio vs. Television

Q1. Compare and contrast the features distinguishing Radio from Television.

  • Auditory vs. Audio-Visual: Radio relies purely on sound (voice, sound effects, music, silence) to create mental pictures ("the theater of the mind"). Television relies on the synergy of sight, sound, motion, and color.

  • Production Complexity & Cost: Radio production is fast, highly portable, and low-cost. TV production requires multi-camera setups, complex lighting, set design, editing, and higher operational budgets.

  • Portability & Accessibility: Radio is highly mobile—listeners consume it while driving, farming, or walking using cheap battery/solar devices. Television traditionally demands fixed, focused visual attention in a set location.

  • Illiteracy Barriers: Both overcome textual illiteracy, but radio excels in rural reach because it requires no visual decoding or complex setup.

  • Intimacy & Personal Connection: Radio is a 1-on-1, intimate medium often consumed individually. TV is frequently a social or group-viewing experience.

Q2. Discuss 5 characteristics of Radio that act as its limitations.

  1. Audio-Only / Blind Medium: The absence of visuals means complex spatial, visual, or structural information is difficult to convey and relies entirely on descriptive narration.

  2. Transient / Ephemeral Nature: Radio messages pass in real-time. Once spoken, the message disappears into the airwaves unless recorded; listeners cannot "rewind" a live traditional broadcast.

  3. Low Retention / Selective Attention: Because radio is often consumed as background noise while multitasking, listener recall is lower compared to visual media.

  4. Perishability of Airtime: Radio inventory is time-bound. An unsold 30-second commercial slot at 8:00 AM is lost forever and cannot be stored or resold later.

  5. Signal Interference / Range Vulnerability: Radio signals (especially AM/Shortwave and local FM) are subject to atmospheric interference, terrain blockages, and signal fading.

Module 3: Scriptwriting & Technicalities (Radio vs. TV)

Q1. What are the key guiding parameters (the 6 Cs) for writing a radio script?

  1. Clarity: Use simple, conversational language; write short sentences to avoid confusion.

  2. Conciseness: Get straight to the point without wordiness or bloated sentences.

  3. Conversational Tone: Write for the ear, not the eye. Use spoken grammar, contractions (e.g., "don't" instead of "do not"), and informal transitions.

  4. Correctness: Ensure factual accuracy, proper names, and verified news details.

  5. Compassion/Human Interest: Frame stories with warmth and emotional resonance to connect with the listener.

  6. Concreteness: Use vivid, descriptive words that evoke clear mental imagery ("theater of the mind").

    • For Audiences: The opportunity cost (time, attention, electricity/data cost) spent consuming the broadcast.

    • For Advertisers: The rate card charges for commercial spots, sponsorships, and hype reads.

  3. Place: The distribution and transmission reach of the station. This includes frequency strength (FM/AM coverage), channel assignment, signal clarity, and digital presence (streaming apps, satellite positioning like DSTV channel numbers).

  4. Promotion: The marketing and publicity methods used to build station awareness. Includes cross-programme teasers, billboards, social media campaigns, roadshows, and listener contests.

Enumerate and discuss the 3 stages of production in a broadcast studio.

Q2. Explain 5 major Programme Scheduling Strategies.

  1. Hammocking: Placing a new or weak programme between two established, highly-rated popular programmes to boost its viewership.

  2. Tentpoling: Placing a major hit show in the middle of a programming block (e.g., 8:00 PM) to pull audiences into the weaker shows airing before and after it.

  3. Stripping: Scheduling the same programme at the exact same time slot every weekday (e.g., airing a soap opera at 7:00 PM Monday through Friday) to build viewer habits.

  4. Counter-Programming: Offering content completely different from a competitor’s lead show at the same time slot (e.g., airing a sports panel show against a rival station's romantic soap opera).

  5. Bridging: Starting a programme slightly off the standard hour mark (e.g., starting at 7:05 PM instead of 7:00 PM) or extending a hit show past the top of the hour to prevent viewers from switching to a rival station's new show.

Module 8: Historical Evolution of Broadcasting in Nigeria

Q1. History of Radio from invention to Rediffusion in Nigeria.

  • Global Invention: Radio evolved through electromagnetic breakthroughs by James Clerk Maxwell, Heinrich Hertz, Guglielmo Marconi (wireless telegraphy, 1895), and Lee de Forest (Audion vacuum tube, 1906).

  • Arrival in Nigeria (1932): Radio entered Nigeria on December 1, 1932, with the establishment of the Radio Distribution Service (RDS) in Lagos by the British Colonial Post and Telegraphs Department.

  • Rediffusion Era: RDS was a wired relay system ("rediffusion") that piped BBC signals from London via cables to speaker boxes installed in subscribers' homes for a monthly fee.

  • Transformation: In 1951, RDS evolved into the Nigerian Broadcasting Service (NBS), which later became the Nigerian Broadcasting Corporation (NBC) by an Act of Parliament in 1956—shifting Nigeria from wired relay to wireless indigenous broadcasting.

Q2. Historical overview of Television growth to its establishment in Nigeria.

  • Western Nigeria First: Television broadcasting in Nigeria (and Africa as a whole) was pioneered on October 31, 1959, with the launch of the Western Nigeria Television (WNTV) in Ibadan by Chief Obafemi Awolowo's regional government. Its motto was "First in Africa."

  • Regional Competition:

    • 1960: Eastern Nigeria Television (ENTV) was established in Enugu.

    • 1962: Radio Television Kaduna (RKTV) was established in the North.

    • 1962: The Federal Government launched the Nigerian Television Service (NTS) in Lagos.

  • Federal Takeover (1977): Under Decree 24 of 1977 (backdated to 1976), the Federal Military Government nationalized all regional TV stations under a single body: the Nigerian Television Authority (NTA).

  • Deregulation (1992): General Ibrahim Babangida promulgated Decree 38 of 1992, establishing the National Broadcasting Commission (NBC) and allowing private ownership of broadcast stations (e.g., Raypower FM, AIT, Channels TV).

    • Limitation: Lacks visual cues. Complex visual or spatial information must be described entirely through spoken words and sound effects, increasing cognitive load on the listener.

  2. Transient Nature (Ephemerality):

    • Limitation: Broadcast signals pass in real time. Once spoken, information disappears immediately unless recorded, preventing listeners from "rewinding" live airwaves.

    • Limitation: Radio is usually consumed while multitasking (driving, working, cooking), leading to lower audience focus and lower message retention compared to visual media.

  4. Perishability of Airtime:

    • Limitation: Radio inventory is strictly time-bound. An unsold 30-second commercial slot at 8:00 AM cannot be stored or resold later; the revenue opportunity is permanently lost.

  5. Vulnerability to Signal Interference:

    • Limitation: Atmospheric conditions, terrain barriers, high-rise buildings, and technical faults can easily degrade signal quality or cause audio static.
