PART A: CONSOLIDATED REVISION QUESTIONS & MODEL ANSWERS (MERGED FROM PAST QUESTIONS & TMAs)
MODULE 1: THE IMPERATIVE AND PROCESS OF ADVERTISING PLANNING
Q1: Justify why advertising planning is imperative and acts as a 'guide' and 'guardian' in contemporary practice. Why is haphazard planning dangerous?
•	Core Answer / Justification:


o	Advertising planning is not a child of necessity; it is a fundamental operational prerequisite that prevents resource wastage, target miss-alignment, and campaign failure.

o	Acts as a Guide: It provides a clear roadmap, setting objectives, budgeting parameters, creative directions, and media schedules so that all agency tiers work toward unified goals.

o	Acts as a Guardian: It protects the brand and financial investment against market volatility, consumer backlash, and regulatory violations.

o	Dangers of Haphazard Planning: Haphazard planning leads to misapplication of scarce resources, wrong audience targeting, conflicting creative messages, and eventual campaign collapse.

MODULE 2: PROCESSES OF ADVERTISING
Q2: Identify and discuss the core steps involved in the process of an advertising campaign / programme.
•	Core Answer / Steps:


1.	Market Analysis & Situation Audit: Examining the product, target audience, competitive landscape, and environmental factors.

2.	Setting Objectives & Budgeting: Determining what the campaign aims to achieve (awareness, trial, loyalty) and establishing financial limits.

3.	Target Audience Definition: Segmenting and profiling the exact demographic and psychographic group to reach.

4.	Creative Strategy Formulation (The Big Idea): Developing the core message platform, appeals, and copywriting execution angles.

5.	Media Planning & Selection: Choosing the optimal media vehicles (broadcast, print, digital, outdoor) to deliver the message efficiently.

6.	Campaign Execution: Production and actual placement of ads across media channels according to the media schedule.

7.	Post-Campaign Evaluation: Measuring campaign effectiveness against set objectives through research and tracking.

MODULE 3: THE ADVERTISING TRIPOD & MARKETING MIX
Q3: Discuss the concept of the Advertising Tripod and the relationship/roles of its three key elements.
•	Core Answer:


o	The advertising tripod consists of three cardinal elements: The Advertiser (Client), The Advertising Agency, and The Media.

o	The Advertiser: Initiates the process, defines product utility, provides the marketing brief, and funds the campaign.

o	The Advertising Agency: Acts as the professional expert that plans, designs, creates, and executes the campaign strategy on behalf of the advertiser.

o	The Media: The delivery vehicles that transport the commercial message from the creator to the target audience.

o	Interrelationship: The success of an advertising programme depends on the symbiotic synergy of all three items; if any leg of the tripod is ignored or functions poorly, the entire structure collapses, leading to campaign failure.

Q4: Examine the components of the Marketing Mix (4Ps) and their usefulness to advertising planning.
•	Core Answer / 4Ps:


1.	Product: What is being sold (features, quality, design, packaging, branding). Dictates the core unique selling proposition (USP) featured in the advertisement.

2.	Price: The monetary value placed on the product. Influences whether the advertising tone should focus on luxury/prestige or economy/value.

3.	Place (Distribution): Channels through which the product reaches consumers. Guides the geographic reach and timing of media scheduling.

4.	Promotion: Comprises advertising, sales promotion, PR, and personal selling. Advertising is the paid mass-media arm that builds brand equity and drives pull strategy.

MODULE 4: REGULATORY BODIES & SECTORAL ASSOCIATIONS
Q5: Evaluate the functions, statutory strengths, and weaknesses/constraints of ARCON (formerly APCON) in regulating Nigerian advertising.
•	Core Answer:


o	Functions/Mandate: Regulating and controlling advertising practice in all its aspects, determining standards of knowledge/skill, and approving advertisements for sensitive categories (e.g., lottery, alcohol, tobacco).

o	Statutory Strengths: Backed by various legislations providing legal powers to register practitioners, prosecute illegal practitioners, and sanction erring agencies or media houses.

o	Weaknesses/Constraints: Stretches across Nigeria’s vast economic and geographical expanse, often under-resourced, facing enforcement hurdles against rogue practitioners and digital space infractions.

Q6: Identify and discuss the roles of major sectoral bodies in the Nigerian advertising industry (AAAN, ADVAN, NPAN, OAAN, BON, MIPAN).
•	Core Answer:


o	AAAN (Association of Advertising Agencies of Nigeria): Represents registered agency owners, standardizing creative practice and ethics.

o	ADVAN (Advertisers Association of Nigeria): Represents the clients/brands funding advertising, protecting corporate marketing interests.

o	NPAN (Newspaper Proprietors Association of Nigeria): Represents print media publishers, ensuring print standards and business viability.

o	OAAN (Out-of-home Advertising Association of Nigeria): Manages outdoor media operators and billboard standards.

o	BON (Broadcasting Organisation of Nigeria): Umbrella body for radio and TV stations, born partly out of historical broadcasting evolution.

o	MIPAN (Media Independent Practitioners Association of Nigeria): Represents specialized media planning and buying agencies.

MODULE 5: ADVERTISING MEDIA (PRINT, BROADCAST, OUTDOOR, DIGITAL)
Q7: Discuss the strengths (advantages) and demerits (disadvantages) of Newspapers and Magazines as advertising tools.
•	Core Answer:


o	Newspaper Strengths: High credibility, wide geographic reach, reader flexibility, detailed product information, and permanence/reference value.

o	Newspaper Demerits (Weaknesses): Short shelf life (discarded daily), poor print reproduction quality on newsprint, cluttered advertising space, and low readership among younger demographics.

o	Magazine Strengths: Excellent visual reproduction color quality, highly targeted specialized readership, long shelf life/retention in homes, and high prestige value.

o	Magazine Demerits: Long lead times for ad submission, high production costs, and lack of daily immediacy.

Q8: Analyze the peculiar features, merits, and demerits of Radio, Television, and Outdoor (Billboard) advertising.
•	Core Answer:


o	Radio Merits: Overcomes illiteracy through audio, highly portable, cost-effective production, reaches remote rural populations, and local language flexibility.

o	Radio Demerits: Fleeting/transient message (no visual or review option), background noise distraction, and clutter.

o	Television Merits: Multi-sensory impact (sight, sound, motion, color), high emotional engagement, and excellent product demonstration capability.

o	Television Demerits: Extremely high production and airtime costs, zapping/zipping behavior during commercial breaks, and brief exposure time.

o	Outdoor (Billboard) Merits: High frequency and repeated exposure, strategic geographic placement, 24/7 visibility, and local market impact.

o	Outdoor Demerits: Limited message length (must be read in seconds), vulnerability to weather elements, visual pollution criticisms, and high vandalism risks.

MODEL ANSWERS: MAC 331 PRACTICE EXAMINATION PAPER
QUESTION 1
a. With the aid of a clearly described diagram or conceptual model, explain the three cardinal elements of the Advertising Tripod and analyze the collaborative relationship necessary for a campaign's success.


•	The Advertising Tripod Model: The structure of advertising relies on three foundational, interdependent legs known as the Advertising Tripod: The Advertiser (Client), The Advertising Agency, and The Media.

•	The Advertiser: This is the client, manufacturer, or service provider who conceives the product, funds the marketing budget, and provides the initial marketing brief outlining what needs to be communicated.

•	The Advertising Agency: This is the professional, creative organization hired by the advertiser to research the market, formulate the creative strategy, design the messages, and manage the execution of the campaign.

•	The Media: These are the communication channels (print, broadcast, outdoor, digital) that serve as vehicles to transport the commercial message from the creator to the target audience.

•	Collaborative Relationship: Just like a physical tripod, if any of the three legs is missing, weak, or misaligned, the entire structure collapses. The advertiser provides the resources and product vision; the agency translates this into persuasive creative art and strategy; and the media ensures optimal audience delivery. Their relationship must be highly symbiotic and coordinated for the advertising programme to succeed.

b. Justify why skipping formal planning results in resource misapplication and campaign failure in contemporary Nigerian advertising practice.


•	Prevents Resource Wastage: Formal planning ensures that financial resources, time, and creative energy are channeled strictly toward defined target audiences rather than scattered haphazardly across unproductive media. 

•	Provides a Strategic Roadmap: Planning acts as a 'guide' and 'guardian', establishing clear benchmarks, objectives, and execution timelines so all agency departments operate with synchronized focus. 

•	Mitigates Market Risk: In a competitive and volatile economy like Nigeria's, consumer preferences shift rapidly. Structured planning involves thorough market research and situation audits, shielding brands from costly consumer rejection and regulatory infractions.

•	Measures Return on Investment (ROI): Planning establishes clear evaluation criteria, enabling advertisers to track whether campaign goals (awareness, trial, loyalty) were successfully achieved.

QUESTION 2
a. Itemize and critically discuss any five (5) sequential steps involved in the professional process of planning and executing an advertising campaign.


1.	Market Analysis & Situation Audit: Examining the macro-environment, competitive landscape, product strengths, weaknesses, and consumer behavior patterns to identify market opportunities.

2.	Setting Campaign Objectives & Budgeting: Defining precise, measurable goals (e.g., building brand awareness, changing consumer attitudes, or driving sales) and allocating adequate financial resources to achieve them.

3.	Target Audience Definition: Segmenting the market demographically, psychographically, and behaviorally to isolate the exact consumer group toward whom the message will be directed.

4.	Creative Strategy & Message Formulation (The Big Idea): Developing the unique selling proposition (USP), tone of voice, copy platforms, and visual concepts that will capture audience attention and persuade them.

5.	Media Planning & Selection: Analyzing available media vehicles to select the optimal mix of print, broadcast, digital, or outdoor channels that delivers the message to the target audience with maximum reach and frequency.

b. Explain how the marketing mix (4Ps) guides an advertising strategist in framing a product's unique selling proposition (USP).


•	Product: The physical attributes, quality, packaging, and brand personality dictate the core benefit or USP that advertising must highlight (e.g., superior durability or innovative features).

•	Price: The pricing strategy dictates consumer expectations; a premium luxury price requires an advertising tone focused on prestige and status, whereas an economy pricing model demands a focus on value and affordability.

•	Place (Distribution): The distribution channels dictate where the product is available, ensuring that media geographical scheduling aligns with retail availability so consumers can easily purchase what they see advertised.

•	Promotion: Advertising is the primary mass-media promotional tool; understanding its relationship with personal selling, sales promotion, and public relations helps the strategist design a cohesive pull strategy that drives consumer demand.

QUESTION 3
a. Newspapers remain a foundational print medium, yet they possess inherent limitations. Identify four (4) demerits of newspaper advertising and explain how they impact consumer reach and message retention.


1.	Short Shelf Life: Newspapers are daily publications usually read once and discarded or recycled immediately, giving advertisements a very brief lifespan and low repeat exposure.

2.	Poor Print Reproduction Quality: Newsprint paper quality and high-speed printing presses often result in grainy, low-contrast black-and-white or muted color images, reducing the visual appeal of aesthetic products.

3.	High Advertising Clutter: Newspaper pages are heavily packed with editorial content and competing ads, making individual advertisements struggle for reader attention.

4.	Low Youth Demographics Reach: Modern younger demographics increasingly consume news via digital screens, resulting in an older, declining readership base for physical newspapers.

b. Enumerate three (3) distinct advantages that make magazines a preferred medium for specialized product campaigns.


1.	Superior Visual Reproduction: Magazines use high-grade glossy paper and advanced printing technology, offering vibrant, crisp color graphics that enhance product appeal (e.g., fashion, cosmetics, automobiles).

2.	Specialized Target Readership: Magazines cater to specific lifestyle, professional, or hobbyist interests (e.g., business, parenting, technology), enabling advertisers to reach precise niche markets without message wastage.

3.	Long Shelf Life and Retention: Magazines are often kept in homes, offices, or waiting rooms for weeks or months, resulting in multiple readers per copy and high cumulative ad exposure.

QUESTION 4
a. Discuss the statutory responsibilities and regulatory powers of the Advertising Regulatory Council of Nigeria (ARCON) in standardizing advertising practice in Nigeria.


•	Regulatory Mandate: ARCON (formerly APCON) is statutorily empowered by law to regulate and control advertising practice in all its aspects and manifestations across Nigeria. 

•	Setting Professional Standards: It determines what standards of knowledge and skill are required to be registered as an advertising practitioner, ensuring only qualified professionals manage campaigns.

•	Vetting and Approval: It operates vetting panels that screen and approve all advertisements—particularly in sensitive sectors like health, alcohol, lottery, and finance—before public exposure to prevent deceptive claims.

•	Disciplinary Powers: It possesses legal powers to investigate professional misconduct, prosecute illegal practitioners, and sanction erring agencies, media houses, or advertisers that violate the Nigerian Code of Advertising Practice.

b. Highlight the collaborative roles played by any three (3) sectoral bodies (such as AAAN, ADVAN, and MIPAN) in supporting industry self-regulation and professional ethics.


•	AAAN (Association of Advertising Agencies of Nigeria): Represents registered corporate advertising agencies, enforcing ethical creative standards, fair pitch practices, and professional competence among member agencies.

•	ADVAN (Advertisers Association of Nigeria): Represents the clients and brand owners who fund advertising, ensuring that marketing investments adhere to consumer protection ethics and fair trade practices.

•	MIPAN (Media Independent Practitioners Association of Nigeria): Represents specialized media planning and buying agencies, ensuring professional transparency, data integrity, and ethical media rate negotiations.

QUESTION 5
a. Compare and contrast the intrinsic characteristics of Radio versus Television as broadcasting tools for advertising in a developing economy like Nigeria.


•	Similarities: Both are broadcast media that overcome textual illiteracy through spoken audio, reach mass audiences simultaneously, and possess high immediacy and local language flexibility.

•	Contrasting Features (Radio): Relies purely on sound (voice, music, sound effects) creating "theater of the mind"; highly portable; operates via cheap batteries or solar power in off-grid rural communities; production is fast and inexpensive.

•	Contrasting Features (Television): Combines sight, sound, motion, and color for powerful multi-sensory emotional impact and product demonstration; requires uninterrupted electricity and stable television sets; production and airtime costs are significantly higher than radio.

b. Outline five (5) major merits of outdoor (billboard) advertising as a supplementary mass medium.


1.	High Frequency and Repeated Exposure: Fixed billboards located along major highways and traffic junctions expose commuters to the same brand message repeatedly over time.

2.	Strategic Geographic Targeting: Advertisers can select specific neighborhoods, commercial districts, or routes to target exact demographic segments.

3.	24/7 Continuous Visibility: Unlike broadcast ads that air briefly or print ads that are put away, outdoor billboards work around the clock, offering uninterrupted brand presence day and night.

4.	Local Market Impact: Excellent for building high brand awareness and reinforcing campaigns launched concurrently on radio, television, or print.

5.	Cost-Effective Long-Term Reach: Once installed, billboards offer a low cost-per-thousand (CPM) exposure rate over extended campaign durations.

QUESTION 6
Write explanatory notes on any three (3) of the following contemporary advertising practice issues:


•	i. The role and value of research in post-campaign advertising evaluation: Research is the life wire of advertising. Post-campaign evaluation uses quantitative surveys and qualitative feedback to measure whether campaign objectives (recall, attitude shift, sales lift) were achieved, preventing future resource misapplication and guiding strategy adjustments. 

•	ii. Handling sensitive product advertising (e.g., alcoholic beverages and tobacco): Sensitive products are subject to strict statutory restrictions by ARCON. Advertisers must include health warning disclaimers (e.g., "Drink Responsibly"), restrict advertising placement to adult viewing/listening hours (watershed periods), and avoid targeting minors or making false medical health claims.

•	iii. Deceptive practices in Nigerian advertising and consumer protection safeguards: Deceptive practices involve exaggerated claims, misleading product demonstrations, hidden fees, or false guarantees. Consumer protection safeguards are enforced by ARCON, the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC), and industry codes to penalize misleading advertisers and protect public interest.

•	iv. The emergence of digital and internet advertising avenues in modern campaign structures: The internet offers targeted advertising through social media platforms, search engines (SEO/SEM), banner ads, and influencer partnerships. It provides precise audience tracking, real-time engagement, and interactive consumer feedback that traditional mass media cannot match.

MAC 323: PHOTOJOURNALISM
CONSOLIDATED MASTER STUDY GUIDE & REVISION PACKAGE
PART A: MASTER QUESTION GUIDE & MODULE BREAKDOWN
MODULE 1: PHOTOGRAPHY AS A PROFESSION
Unit 1: History and Definition of Photography
•	What is Photography?


o	Derived from Greek words photos (light) and graphein (to draw). 

o	Spencer (1973) defines it as the art, science, and practice of creating durable images by recording light or other electromagnetic radiation, either chemically via light-sensitive film or electronically via an image sensor. 

o	Collins English Dictionary defines it as the art, practice, or occupation of taking and printing photographs and making cine films. 

•	Types of Photographs (Based on Subject Awareness):


1.	The Aware Photograph: Arranged and planned; the subject is fully conscious that their picture is being taken. 

2.	The Semi-Aware Photograph: The subject is partially aware or adjusts posture in the presence of the photographer, though the exact moment of the click is unknown. 

3.	The Candid Photograph: Factual, unarranged, capturing life and action as it unfolds naturally. Requires speed, instinct, and anticipation. 

•	History of Photography:


o	Roots in antiquity with the camera obscura principle. Mo Ti (5th Century BC China) and Greek philosophers Aristotle and Euclid observed pinhole image inversion. 

o	Hercules Florence (1832) studied permanent pinhole image fixing, naming it "photographia," but failed to publish widely. Sir John F.W. Herschel coined the word "photography" in 1839. 

o	Joseph Nicéphore Niépce produced the oldest surviving permanent photograph (Heliograph / sun print) on a polished plate in 1827. 

o	Louis Daguerre partnered with Niépce and invented the daguerreotype in 1839, fixing images on silver-plated copper sheets soaked in iodine. 

Unit 2: Photography: A Profession or Craft
•	Why Photography Matters in Human Life:


1.	Part of our Legacy: Freezes fleeting moments that outlive us, serving as visual pieces of our personal and societal history. 

2.	Sharing and Communication: Fulfills the human desire to share beauty, experiences, and perspectives with strangers and loved ones globally. 

3.	Expression of Art: Allows creators to express emotional and aesthetic viewpoints through visual mediums. 

4.	Complex Language: Provides a visual vocabulary to convey complex human emotions, joy, sorrow, and psychological states when words fail. 

5.	Power to Move Us: Captures attention instantly and speaks directly to human emotions and empathy. 

•	Reasons for Studying Photography:


o	Employment Opportunities: Continuous demand for professional photographers across diverse industries. 

o	Overcoming Boredom: Fieldwork prevents individuals from being confined to standard desk jobs. 

o	Financial Flexibility: Lucrative career paths with freelancing options allowing self-managed rates and hours. 

o	Creativity: Offers infinite outlets for artistic self-expression. 

•	Photography in Nigeria: Traced through archives like the Eliot Elisofon Photographic Archives, early postcards, and pioneer local photographers such as H. Sanya Freeman (Photographer-in-Chief to the Governor of Nigeria circa 1910), Neils Walwin Holm, and Solomon Osagie Alonge. 

Unit 3: Professional Photographers
•	Who is a Professional Photographer? An individual who engages in photography as a primary source of income and livelihood. They may be full-time newspaper/magazine employees, contracted event coverage specialists, or independent freelancers (including paparazzi and fine art photographers). 

•	Copyright and Legal Protection in Photography: The author of a photographic negative/file owns the copyright. Commercial users purchase licenses or usage rights via contracts specifying territory, duration, and frequency, distinct from production fees. 

Unit 4: Types and Forms of Pictures in Mass Media
•	Functions of Pictures in Mass Media:


1.	Credibility: Makes events seem real and true, lending undeniable authenticity to news reports. 

2.	Visual Appeal: Acts as visual magnets and entry points, breaking layout monotony with contrasting tones. 

3.	Vitality: Adds life and human-interest energy to stories in ways words cannot match. 

4.	Aesthetics: Introduces beauty and artistic appeal to publication pages. 

•	News Photojournalism: The art of reporting news in pictures, divided into hard news photographs (disasters, wars, major government events) and soft news photographs (ceremonies, foundation layings, seminars). 

MODULE 2: THE CORE OF PHOTOJOURNALISM
Unit 1: A Professional Photo-Journalist
•	Distinction between Photographer and Photojournalist: While traditional photographers capture static "nouns" (people, places, things), photojournalists hunt and capture active "verbs" (explodes, cries, kicks, protests) that tell a developing story. 

•	Types of Photojournalists:


1.	Commercial Photojournalist: Specialized in advertising; operates under intense pressure to produce creative studio setups, props, and still-life shots for brands. 

2.	Fashion Photojournalist: Focuses on style, clothing, accessories, and models, requiring interpersonal tact to guide and relax subjects. 

3.	Industrial Photojournalist: Employed in-plant or freelance by corporations to cover AGMs, factory operations, industrial manuals, and staff events. 

4.	Freelance Photojournalist: Offers independent services for hire across diverse market sectors. 

5.	Press Photojournalist: Possesses a sharp "news nose," operating with absolute speed to capture breaking events before print deadlines. 

•	Qualities and Attributes of a Professional Photojournalist: Possesses artistic and technical skills, patience, high concentration, emotional stability, ability to interpret editors' ideas, strict adherence to ethics, and respect for legal boundaries. 

Unit 2: The Place of Photography in Journalism
•	Visual reporting acts as an indispensable partner to text, bridging literacy gaps and providing multi-coded memory traces that enhance information recall and emotional engagement among readers. 

MODULE 3: PHOTO EDITING AND PUBLICATION
Unit 1: Photo Editing: Meaning and Origin
•	Definition: Photo editing is the process of selecting, cropping, adjusting, and refining crude photographic shots to make them fit for publication, ensure clarity, uphold moral values, and protect against legal liabilities. 

•	Reasons for Photo Editing:


1.	Clarity: Correcting blurriness, poor lighting, low contrast, or submerged backgrounds caused by camera limitations or poor environmental angles. 

2.	Specific Targets: Focusing attention on relevant details while masking distracting background elements. 

3.	Moral Value: Eliminating obscene, indecent, or dehumanizing elements that violate societal standards. 

4.	Beautification: Enhancing aesthetic presentation and correcting technical defects like "red-eye". 

5.	Legal Implications: Blurring faces of unconvicted suspects or editing backgrounds to prevent defamatory associations (libel). 

Unit 2: Editing Concepts in Photo-Journalism (1)
•	Captioning (Cutline): A concise descriptive block accompanying an image that answers the 5 Ws (Who, What, When, Where, Why/How). Good captions avoid trite phrases ("looks on"), refrain from editorializing or guessing emotions, reflect the exact image, and maintain strict spelling accuracy. 

•	Blurring: A technique where parts of an image are faded or covered to protect personal dignity, privacy, moral values, or legal compliance. 

Unit 3: Editing Concepts in Photo-Journalism (2)
•	Scaling, Cutting (Cropping), and Fixing: Techniques used to resize photographs to fit specific page layout dimensions. Methods include Electronic Terminals, Proportion Wheels, the Ratio Method ($\text{RW} \div \text{OW} \times \text{OD} = \text{RD}$), and the Diagonal Method. Cropping removes unwanted details to improve composition. 

•	Bleeding: Running photographs intentionally off one or more trim edges of a page with no surrounding margin. 

•	Flopping: Mirror-reversing an image across a vertical axis to improve aesthetic appeal, adapt to right/left-hand drive markets, direct a subject's gaze into text columns, or respect cultural taboos (e.g., avoiding left-hand eating depictions in Muslim societies). 

•	Retouching: Correcting physical blemishes, red-eye, and smoothing skin tones in portrait images. 

Unit 4: Computer and Adobe Photo Shop
•	Photoshopping: Using digital software programs (like Adobe Photoshop) to edit images. While legitimate for technical enhancement, unethical manipulation creates visual parodies or digital fakery intended to deceive. 

•	Microsoft Office Picture Manager: A basic image management tool supporting cropping, resizing, color fine-tuning, red-eye removal, and batch renaming. 

MODULE 4: PHOTOGRAPHY TOOLS
Unit 1: Camera types and parts
•	Camera Types:


o	Point and Shoot (P&S): Automatic focus, fixed/built-in features, lightweight, ideal for casual snapshots. 

o	SLR and DSLR (Single Lens Reflex / Digital Single Lens Reflex): Interchangeable lenses, through-the-lens (TTL) viewing, manual and automatic control over exposure, shutter speed, and aperture. 

o	Compact/Digital Cameras: Versatile range from camera phones to ultra-compact pocket devices and bridge cameras. 

•	Key Camera Parts & Uses:


o	Body: The foundational housing enclosing the film/sensor, battery, and internal controls. 

o	Lens: Focuses incoming light onto the film or digital sensor via adjustable apertures. 

o	Viewfinder: The sighting window used by the photographer to frame and compose shots (TTL viewfinders use mirrors for absolute accuracy). 

o	Shutter Release: The button that activates the mechanical/electronic shutter to expose the sensor to light. 

o	Shutter / Shutter Speed Control: Controls the duration light reaches the sensor, measured in fractions of a second (e.g., $1/60$ s). 

o	Film Speed Control (ISO): Calibrates the camera meter to light sensitivity. 

o	F-Stop Control: Regulates the aperture size within the lens. 

o	Hot Shoe Mount: The electrical contact bracket on top of SLR cameras for attaching external flashes.

•	Core Ethical Principles (NPPA & Course Standards):


1.	Accuracy: Maintain factual records of time, date, and circumstances; never stage-manage or artificially manipulate shoots; avoid editing beyond technical necessity. 

2.	Impartiality: Remain objective, avoid personal bias, and represent all sides of a story fairly.

4.	Privacy & Consent: Respect personal privacy, especially concerning vulnerable subjects and victims of grief.

6.	Professional Dignity: Refuse bribes, gifts, or financial favours in exchange for biased coverage.

•	Copyright Law: Protects original photographic works, vesting ownership in the creator (or employing media organization), prohibiting unauthorized reproduction. 

•	Obscenity: Publishing materials that tend to deprave, corrupt, or violate societal moral standards is a punishable offence. 

•	Sedition: Publishing photographs intended to incite hatred, disaffection, or violent revolt against government officials or promote hostility between population classes. 

•	Invasion of Privacy: Protects individuals against unauthorized intrusion, publication of embarrassing private facts, misappropriation of likeness, and false light distortion.

PART A: MASTER QUESTION GUIDE & MODULE BREAKDOWN
MODULE 1: FOUNDATIONS OF CRITICAL WRITING & THINKING
Unit 1: Meaning of Critical Writing
•	Definition of Criticism: An appraisal, evaluation, or judgment offered on a piece of art, performance, or production, which may be positive, negative, or both. It requires a deeper knowledge of the art and an understanding of standards of measurement. C.T. Winchester defines it as "the intelligent appreciation of any work of art, and by consequence, the just estimate of its value and rank". 

•	Four Basic Meanings of Criticism (Pope, 2002: 43):


1.	Finding fault and pulling to pieces in a negative sense. 

2.	Analyzing and pulling to pieces in the neutral sense of taking apart. 

3.	Interpreting with a view to establishing meaning and understanding. 

4.	Evaluating with a view to establishing relative or absolute worth. 

•	What it Means to be Critical: At the university level, being critical does not mean finding fault negatively; rather, it means questioning information and opinions, examining subjects from multiple perspectives, and applying theoretical frameworks. It involves appreciation (aesthetic perception), analysis (breaking content into components to see interrelations), and evaluation (rating a work based on defined criteria and purpose). 

Unit 2: Critical Writing and Critical Thinking
•	Definition of Critical Thinking: The intellectually disciplined process of actively and skillfully conceptualizing, applying, analyzing, synthesizing, and/or evaluating information gathered from observation, experience, reflection, reasoning, or communication. Dewey (1933) describes it as active, persistent, and careful consideration of any belief or supposed knowledge in light of its supporting grounds. 

•	Core Skills Shared by Critical Thinking and Critical Writing (Cottrell, 2005; Allen, 2004):


1.	Analysis: Breaking material into component parts to examine structure, relationships, and categories. 

2.	Synthesis: Weaving together material from multiple sources and prior knowledge to create a coherent new whole. 

3.	Interpretation: Comprehending and expressing the meaning, significance, or implications of data and experiences. 

4.	Evaluation: Assessing the credibility of statements and the logical strength of arguments. 

5.	Inference: Identifying implicit elements, drawing reasonable conclusions, and forming hypotheses. 

6.	Explanation: Stating and justifying reasoning in terms of evidence, concepts, and criteria through cogent arguments. 

7.	Self-Regulation: Applying analytical and evaluative skills to one's own judgments to validate or correct reasoning. 

•	Mental Equipment Required by a Critic (Wolseley, 1973): A measure of objectivity, realization of the climate of opinion, awareness of methods of estimation, and perspective gained through knowledge of the art and understanding of the audience. 

Unit 3: Critical Writing and Other Forms of Writing
•	Critical Writing vs. Broadcast Commentary: Both express opinions and seek to influence, but critical writing specifically evaluates what makes a work of art unique using established standards, whereas commentaries point out anomalies, explain processes, or tell stories without necessarily engaging in artistic appreciation. 

•	Critical Writing vs. Editorials: Editorials represent the corporate voice of a medium on public issues to inform, explain, and persuade. Critical writing does this for artistic works while also offering entertainment and guidance to consumers. 

•	Critical Writing vs. News Writing: Traditional news is a straightforward, objective, and timely recounting of facts devoid of personal opinion. Critical writing concentrates on the results of an artist's efforts and emphasizes subjective evaluation. 

•	Critical Writing vs. Feature Writing: Features are creative journalistic articles focusing on human interest, personality sketches, and background stories about artists, but they do not ordinarily evaluate artistic worth. 

•	Critical Writing vs. Column Writing: Columns express the regular opinions of a specialist or columnist on diverse matters. A critic frequently maintains a specialized review column, thus sharing direct overlap with columnists. 

MODULE 2: FUNCTIONS, THEORIES, AND APPROACHES TO CRITICISM
Unit 1: Functions of Criticism
•	The Function of Information: Holding up a mirror to what has been composed or performed, reporting accurately on artistic creations (Oscar Thompson in Wolseley, 1959). 

•	The Function of Influence: Acting as a guide by telling consumers what a work is about, offering an expert opinion, and determining if it is worth their time and money. 

•	The Function of Promotion: Serving as a publicity tool to develop a favorable public attitude toward the work of art. 

•	The Function of Advertising: Attracting commercial advertising accounts and generating direct revenue for the media house, though this can sometimes create ethical tensions. 

•	The Function of Prestige: Lending intellectual and cultural distinction to a publication or broadcast platform among elite audiences. 

Unit 2: Theories of Criticism
1.	The Authoritarian Theory: Based on the belief that fixed, objective standards exist for judging works of art. Authoritarian critics judge new works against historical, moral, judicial, classical, and scientific models from the past. They are rigorous, disciplined, and demand strict conformity to established rules of excellence. 

2.	The Impressionistic Theory: Rejects fixed rules and standards in favor of the critic's personal subjective experience. Following Anatole France's definition, the impressionistic critic "narrates the adventures of his soul among masterpieces". It evaluates a work based on its unique effect upon the critic's sensibility rather than historical conformity. 

Unit 3: Approaches to Criticism (Scott, 1977)
1.	Moral Approach: Utilizes ethical principles of human conduct (order, restraint, discipline) to evaluate how a work reflects or instructs human life. 

2.	Psychological Approach: Employs psychological frameworks (such as psychoanalysis) and examines the author's life and mental motivations to explain characters and creative acts. 

3.	Sociological Approach: Presupposes the deep interrelatedness of art and society, evaluating a work within its historical, economic, and social context. 

4.	Formalistic Approach (New Criticism): Focuses on a close, rigorous textual or aesthetic study of the work itself, independent of external social, political, or biographical ideas. 

5.	Archetypal (Mythological/Ritualistic) Approach: Examines basic cultural patterns, myths, and universal symbols from which a work draws deep human resonance and appeal. 

MODULE 3: COMPONENTS OF CRITICISM
Unit 1: Components of Criticism: The Critic (The Human Factor)
•	The Critic's Background: Encompasses formal training, broad knowledge of art history, philosophy, sociology, acquired tastes, language mastery, and an analytical mind. Building this background requires dedication and extensive exposure to standards of excellence. 

•	The Critic's Attitude: Requires emotional stability, self-awareness of moods, and strict detachment from personal prejudices (such as marital quarrels or bad days). A critic must maintain open-mindedness—willingness to understand the obscure or off-beat—without lapsing into vacuity or dogmatism. 

Unit 2: Components of Criticism: Direct Data (The Work Itself)
Adapted from Oscar Thompson's framework, direct data represents everything physically and intrinsically present in the work of art: 


1.	Material: The physical medium itself (the text of a book, wood, canvas, pigments, stone, or theatrical costumes). 

2.	Form: The structural category or label (e.g., tragedy, comedy, symphony, poetry, prose, biography) and how its structural parts interrelate. 

3.	Workmanship: The craftsmanship, technical skill, originality, inspiration, and conviction with which the artist executes the material. 

Unit 3: Components of Criticism: Indirect Data (Extrinsic Factors)
Indirect data stems entirely from outside the work itself: 


•	The Artist's Intention: What the creator stated, disavowed, or implied about their goals. 

•	The Artist's Output: The position of the work within the creator's broader career trajectory. 

•	Historical & Contemporary Setting: The socio-historical significance of the work and how it compares to contemporary creations. 

•	Caution: While helpful for context, indirect data must not override the intrinsic merit of the work itself. 

MODULE 4: THE CRAFT OF WRITING REVIEWS AND CRITICAL ARTICLES
Unit 1: Writing a Review
•	Nature of a Review: A short piece of critical writing for newspapers or magazines about a specific art event. It blends objective news reporting with subjective evaluation. 

•	Structural Formula for Beginners:


o	The Lead: Captures the 5 Ws and H (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How) using an engaging summary or news-peg. 

o	The Body: Balances reporting with critical analysis in decreasing order of importance, integrating direct and indirect quotes. 

o	The Ending: Concludes with a wrap-up evaluation, a final major point, or a clinching summary. 

•	Copy Preparation Rules: Take extensive notes, respect publication length limits (300–600 words), use third-person pronouns to maintain objective authority, select precise vocabulary, and avoid libellous personal attacks on artists. 

Unit 2: Writing a Critical Article
•	Nature of a Critical Article: A more extensive, intensive, and penetrating prose composition than a review. It evaluates an entire body of work, a complete artistic career, or a broad field. It requires the writer to be idea-minded rather than merely thing-minded. 

•	Seven Steps in Writing a Critical Article:


1.	Receive or conceptualize a clear topic/idea. 

2.	Conduct thorough research via observation, interviews, and literature. 

3.	Analyze and reflect upon findings to discover new insights. 

4.	Develop an organized outline grouping related points. 

5.	Craft an engaging opening (news-peg or anecdote) and a thematic conclusion. 

6.	Present arguments using the deductive method (general to particular) or inductive method (particular to general). 

7.	Draft, revise, edit, and polish the copy. 

Unit 3: The Reviewer and the Critic
•	The Reviewer: Operates primarily as an objective reporter covering a timely art event. Their virtues are speed, topicality, wit, and factual accuracy, helping readers decide whether to attend or buy. 

•	The Critic: Operates as a philosophical analyst who publishes after deeper meditation. Their virtues are profound insight, a firm historical sense, and absolute truth, aiming to shape reader tastes and long-term artistic understanding. 

Unit 4: The Critic's Style
•	Journalistic Style Requirements: Conforms to press traditions using short sentences, simple words, brief paragraphs, and avoidance of unnecessary jargon, while retaining freedom to use specialized artistic terminology. 

•	Tone and Cultivation: Must avoid pomposity, feigned radicalism, excessive wisecracks, and trite clichés. Good style shows no sign of effort; it is achieved through extensive reading, lifelong writing practice, and honest self-expression. 

MODULE 5: REVIEWING SPECIFIC ART GENRES
Unit 1: Reviewing the Fine Arts (Painting and Sculpture)
•	Challenges: Painting and sculpture are highly subjective visual arts lacking fixed verbal texts. Reviewers must evaluate color quality, pigments, brushwork, framing, lighting, presentation, and artistic philosophy. 

•	Methodology: Apply the material-form-workmanship formula, investigate the artist's originality against historical precedents, and report on exhibition logistics (galleries, dates, hours) using a straight news framework. 

Unit 2: Reviewing Books
•	Methodology: Maintain an active reading notebook, record page references for striking passages, and classify the book (biography, fiction, history). 

•	Historical/General Criteria Questions:


1.	Does it establish factual details through strict historical method? 

2.	Does it possess a philosophy or frame of reference of more than transient significance? 

3.	Is it written in a clear style? 

4.	Does it present new data or interpretations rather than mere hackwork? 

5.	Does the author address universal human questions? 

Unit 3: Reviewing Stage Play or Drama
•	Methodology: Assess whether the script is new, provide a brief synopsis and early verdict, evaluate plot universality, characterization (true-to-life vs. caricature), dramatic pacing, stage direction, and technical credits (lighting, costume, set design). Balance personal opinions with fair credit to producers, directors, and actors. 

Unit 4: Reviewing Music
•	Methodology: Account for the ephemeral and subjective nature of music across genres (pop, classical, highlife, jazz, reggae). Evaluate technical perfection, sound balancing, mixing, acoustic presence, recording quality (live concert vs. studio stereo), and interpretative fidelity compared to previous recordings. 

Unit 5: Reviewing Television and Motion Pictures
•	Motion Pictures: Evaluate two-dimensional camera work, mobile framing, special effects (substance vs. gimmick), plot integrity, and characterization within cinematic conventions. 

•	Television Programs: Evaluate adherence to strict time limits, appropriateness of broadcast time slots, commercial placement disruptions, audio volume consistency, program cycles (situation comedies, documentaries), and target audience satisfaction.

While useful for context, it can mislead if a prominent creator's name biases the critic into praising a flawed work instead of judging it on its intrinsic merits. 

QUESTION 4 MODEL ANSWERS:
•	a. Critical Writing vs. News & Features: News is an objective, straightforward recounting of timely facts without personal opinion. Features are colorful human-interest stories about artists and life without evaluative mandates. Critical writing uniquely combines reporting with subjective judgment, aesthetic appreciation, and consumer guidance. 

•	b. Four Steps in Writing a Critical Article: (1) Idea conception and topic selection; (2) Research and data gathering from interviews and observations; (3) Analysis, reflection, and outlining of arguments; (4) Drafting using deductive/inductive methods followed by rigorous rewriting and editing. 

QUESTION 5 MODEL ANSWERS:
•	a. Reviewer vs. Critic: Reviewers are objective news reporters focusing on timely art events with virtues of speed, wit, and factual accuracy. Critics are philosophical analysts publishing after long meditation, possessing a profound historical sense and striving for absolute truth to shape cultural tastes. 

•	b. Five Book Review Questions: (1) Does it establish factual details through strict historical method?; (2) Does it have a philosophy of more than transient significance?; (3) Is the style clear and helpful?; (4) Does it present new data rather than hackwork?; (5) Does it address universal human questions?.
